Part VI — The nard family: foliatum, spicatum, nardinum

What this is. A testimonia dossier for the three names the ancient market gave the nard perfumes — νάρδινον (nardinon), the Greek trade name; φουλιᾶτον / foliatum, the perfume named for the leaf; and σπικᾶτον / spicatum, named for the spike. It belongs beside Parts I–II because the Mendesian’s own lexical tradition runs through it: Erotian reports the Hippocratic “Egyptian perfume” as “called by some Malabathrinon, by others the Mendesian” (see the opening section), and the malabathron leaf is precisely what separates foliatum from plain nardinon. Theophrastus, four centuries earlier, already lists τὸ αἰγύπτιον and τὸ νάρδινον side by side as the perfumes women wore. Aëtius of Amida preserves the recipes for all three names, across two books.

Every Greek and Latin word quoted below was copied from a verified witness file and checked against it, never typed from memory; the provenance and verification tier of each is stated in the closing note. Identifications stay at the level of the ancient lexicon — no modern species is imposed on νάρδος.

Name Greek / Latin What distinguishes it Recipe survives?
Nardinon νάρδινον; nardinum The base trade name: an oil charged with nard and its companions. Made with the malabathron leaf or without it — the fork that generates the other two names. Yes — Dioscorides MM 1.62; Pliny NH 13.15; and a workshop recipe in Aëtius I

Spicatum σπικᾶτον, σπεκᾶτον; spicatum The spike perfume — Latin spica = ναρδόσταχυς, the nard spike. Named repeatedly by Galen alongside foliatum. Yes — Aëtius XVI, under the spelling ΣΠΕΚΑΤΟΥ

The names mark emphasis, not exclusivity. It would be natural to read foliatum as the leaf perfume and spicatum as the spike perfume and assume each lacks the other’s ingredient. The recipes say otherwise: Aëtius’ spicatum contains both ναρδόσταχυς (spike) and φύλλον (leaf), and his second foliatum contains both as well. The two names name the characteristic element of a shared formula, not two separate materials.

And the names are Roman — Galen says so. Both enter Greek as loanwords wearing Latin dress, and Galen flags the borrowing every time: “what they call at Rome φουλίατον,” “in the tongue of the Romans,” “the one called in Latin.” At Method of Medicine 8.5 he derives the things from the words — these perfumes “seem to have been invented by the luxury at Rome, and to have their appellations from it.” A contemporary naming the market that produced a product is a rare thing in the perfume record.

The first-century recipe: Dioscorides and Pliny agree

The two independent first-century witnesses give the same formula, ingredient for ingredient, in two languages. Dioscorides names the fork that Pliny’s double title records:

νάρδινον δὲ μύρον ποικίλως σκευάζεται διὰ τοῦ μαλαβαθρίνου φύλλου καὶ δίχα τούτου. ὡς τὸ πολὺ δὲ μείγνυται τῷ βαλανίνῳ ἐλαίῳ ἢ ὀμφακίνῳ, πρὸς μὲν τὴν στύψιν τοῦ ἐλαίου σχοῖνος, πρὸς δὲ τὴν εὐωδίαν βάλλεται κόστος, ἄμωμον, νάρδος, σμύρνα, βάλσαμον.

Nardinon perfume is prepared in various ways, with the malabathron leaf and without it. For the most part it is mixed with balanos oil or omphacine; for the astringing of the oil, aromatic rush; for the fragrance are thrown in costus, amomum, nard, myrrh, balsam.

Seven for seven, plus the leaf. The convergence is not an editor’s harmonization: Wellmann’s own apparatus to this chapter glosses the Greek fork with the Latin name — conficitur cum folio malabatri et sine isto miscetur (foliatum a Pl. l. s. appellatur), “it is made with malabathron leaf and mixed without it (called foliatum by Pliny in the passage cited).” The equation was printed in the critical edition before it was a claim of ours.

And a simple version. Dioscorides closes with a plain grade — σκευάζεται δὲ καὶ λιτὴ δι᾿ ὀμφακίνου ἐλαίου καὶ σχοίνου καὶ καλάμου καὶ κόστου καὶ νάρδου: “a simple kind is also prepared, from omphacine oil and rush and reed and costus and nard” — no leaf, no myrrh, no balsam. The same two-tier structure as the Mendesian and susinum of Part I: a costly compound and a cheap one under one name.

The workshop recipes: Aëtius I and XVI

Aëtius of Amida is the recipe tradition for this whole family. Book I carries a nardinon under a named perfumer’s name; book XVI carries four more in a row — three foliatum and, immediately after them, a spicatum. They are quoted here as printed, with the edition’s line-break hyphens closed up; γο and 𐆄 are the ounce (οὐγγία), λίτ. / λίτραι the litra, ξ̸ the xestes, and the letters are numerals.

0 · Ναρδίνου σκευασία Ἰωάννου μυρεψοῦ — nardinon, by John the perfumer

Ναρδίνου σκευασία Ἰωάννου μυρεψοῦ. Ἐλαίου ξ̸ ε ϛ ἀσπαλάθου λίτραι δ ξυλοβαλσάμου λίτραι β κόστου 𐆄 γʹ ξυλοκασίας 𐆄 δ καρποβαλσάμου 𐆄 γʹ ἀμώμου 𐆄 γʹ στύρακος καλαμίτου 𐆄 β ὀποβαλσάμου 𐆄 β.

Preparation of nardinon, of John the perfumer. Oil, 5–6 xestai; aspalathos, 4 litrai; xylobalsam, 2 litrai; costus, 3 ounces; xylocassia, 4 ounces; carpobalsam, 3 ounces; amomum, 3 ounces; storax kalamites, 2 ounces; opobalsam, 2 ounces.

A named perfumer is a rarity in this material: most recipes travel anonymously or under a physician’s name, and here the compiler credits a working μυρεψός. Note too that this nardinon has no nard in its ingredient list — the name has become a formula-type rather than a description, exactly as foliatum and spicatum are shown to be below.

0b · Ἐλαίου σαλκᾶ σκευασία — the salka named by the foliatum recipe

Immediately after it stands the preparation of salka oil — the very perfume the first foliatum recipe reaches for when the batch comes out too thick — and it opens with a first-person workshop note that places the compiler at the bench:

Ἐλαίου σαλκᾶ σκευασία. Ἐσκεύασα ταύτην ἐν Ἀλεξανδρείᾳ καί ἐστι πάνυ καλλίστη· ἀσπαλάθου 𐆄 ϛʹ ξυλοβαλσάμου 𐆄 θ κυπέρων 𐆄 δ ἑλενίου 𐆄 ϛʹ ἴρεως 𐆄 ϛʹ καλάμου γρ ιη σχοίνου ἄνθους 𐆄 β ς στύρακος λιπαροῦ 𐆄 β κάρυα ἰνδικὰ β φύλλου γρ ιη ναρδοστάχυος 𐆄 α καρυοφύλλου 𐆄 α ς ἀρνάβω 𐆄 α ς ἀμώμου 𐆄 γʹ κασίας 𐆄 β κόστου 𐆄 α σμύρνης 𐆄 α ὕπνου 𐆄 γ ξυλοκασίας 𐆄 γ ἐλαίου ξ̸ ε ι.

Preparation of salka oil. I prepared this in Alexandria, and it is quite excellent. Aspalathos, 6 ounces; xylobalsam, 9; cyperus, 4; elenion, 6; iris, 6; reed, 18 grammata; rush-flower, 2½; oily storax, 2; Indian nuts, 2; leaf, 18 grammata; nard-spike, 1; clove, 1½; arnabō, 1½; amomum, 3; cassia, 2; costus, 1; myrrh, 1; hypnon, 3; xylocassia, 3; oil, 5–10 xestai.

Two things to flag. First, ἀρναβώ — the aromatic Part II lists as unidentified, known only as a permitted substitute for cinnamon in Paul’s susinum — is here weighed into a working formula at an ounce and a half. That is a second attestation for a word the catalogue could previously locate only once. Second, the recipe is dated and placed by its own author: made in Alexandria, and pronounced excellent. Perfume recipes almost never say where they were made.

1 · ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ — the short one

ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ. Ὀποβαλσάμου λίτ. α. στύρακος, ἀμώμου ἀνὰ γο γ. κασίας γο δ. κρίνα τὸν ἀριθμὸν κ. λέαινε ἅμα καὶ χρῶ. εἰ δὲ παχύτερον εἴη, ἔκλυε αὐτὸ μύρῳ σαλκᾶ.

Preparation of foliatum. Opobalsam, 1 litra; storax and amomum, 3 ounces each; cassia, 4 ounces; lilies, twenty in number. Grind together and use. And if it should be too thick, dissolve it with salka perfume.

Twenty lilies in a nard perfume is the surprise — and the link to susinum in Part I. The closing clause is the workshop’s own answer to the defect Galen complains of.

2 · ἄλλη γραφὴ φουλιάτου — the wax-bodied one

“Another writing of foliatum”: wax 1 litra; Cyzicene nard 3 litrai; Brutian pitch 5 oz; ladanum 1 oz; oily storax 3 oz; costus and cassia 2 oz each; cardamom and leaf 1 oz each; nard-spike 1 oz; saffron and Aminaean wine 1 oz each; opobalsam 3 oz. A named regional nard (Cyzicene), a named wine, and both leaf and spike in one formula.

3 · ΕΚ ΤΩΝ ΟΡΕΙΒΑΣΙΟΥ ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ — from Oribasius

ΕΚ ΤΩΝ ΟΡΕΙΒΑΣΙΟΥ ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ. Κασίας γο στ. φύλλου γο γ. ἀμώμου, λαδάνου ἀνὰ γο α. πεπέρεως κόκκους μ. σμύρνης τρωγλίτιδος γο α. νάρδου μύρου λίτ. α. κόψας καὶ σήσας λεπτοτάτῳ κοσκίνῳ λείου πάλιν ἐν ἑτέρῳ ἐπιμελῶς, καὶ ἐπίβαλλε τὴν νάρδον καὶ πάλιν λείου ἐπιμελῶς. πρὸς δὲ τὸ μελάντερον γενέσθαι τὸ φάρμακον, καρύων βασιλικῶν λέπη καύσας καὶ τρίψας ἐν θυίᾳ ἕνωσον, πρόσμιγε δὲ καὶ ὀποβαλσάμου κοχλιάρια γ. καὶ χρῶ πολυτελεστάτῳ μύρῳ καὶ πρὸς παρέσεις.

From the works of Oribasius: preparation of foliatum. Cassia, 6 ounces; leaf, 3 ounces; amomum and ladanum, 1 ounce each; peppercorns, 40 grains; Troglodytic myrrh, 1 ounce; nard perfume, 1 litra. Having pounded and sifted through the finest sieve, grind again carefully in another, and throw in the nard, and grind carefully again. And to make the drug darker, burn the shells of royal nuts and grind them in a mortar and combine; and mix in also 3 spoonfuls of opobalsam. Use it as a most costly perfume, and for pareses.

Three things here are not in any other witness: a deliberate colourant step (burnt shells of the “royal nut”, to darken the finished drug), a dosing spoon (κοχλιάρια), and a double use — the same jar is “a most costly perfume” and a treatment for pareses, partial paralysis. Luxury and pharmacy in one line.

4 · ΣΠΕΚΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ — the spicatum

ΣΠΕΚΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ. Ἀμώμου γο β. σμύρνης τρωγλίτιδος, κόστου ἀνὰ γο α. φύλλου, κασίας, φυλλίνου μύρου ἢ σαλκᾶ ἀνὰ γο γ. ναρδοστάχυος γο α. νάρδου κελτικῆς γο δ. ναρδίνου μύρου γο στ. κηροῦ λευκοῦ τὸ ἀρκοῦν, ὥστε σύστασιν ὑγροτέραν ἔχειν· ὀποβαλσάμου γο γ.

Preparation of spicatum. Amomum, 2 ounces; Troglodytic myrrh and costus, 1 ounce each; leaf, cassia, and leaf-perfume or salka, 3 ounces each; nard-spike, 1 ounce; Celtic nard, 4 ounces; nardinon perfume, 6 ounces; white wax, as much as suffices, so that it has a rather fluid consistency; opobalsam, 3 ounces.

The perfume named for the spike turns out to contain four times as much Celtic nard as spike, and to be built on nardinon perfume as its vehicle — a compound of a compound. And its wax clause is a formulation instruction about consistency: κηροῦ λευκοῦ τὸ ἀρκοῦν, ὥστε σύστασιν ὑγροτέραν ἔχειν, “white wax, as much as suffices, so that it has a rather fluid consistency.” The perfumer is managing the very property Galen found unmanageable.

Who wore it, and why doctors prescribed it

Wealthy women, on the sources’ own account. Every Greek notice of foliatum and spicatum attaches them to rich women — Galen calls them “the costly perfumes of rich women” four separate times, and at Method of Medicine 8.5 “the luxurious perfumes prepared for the indulgence of pampered women.” The framing is hostile, which is itself evidence: moralising writers complain about what is popular. Men wore perfume too, and the same authors say so elsewhere, but on these two names they are near-silent about male users.

Theophrastus explains the preference, and it is physical. Four centuries before Galen he divides the market by weight of scent, and the reason he gives for the women’s list is tenacity: διὰ γὰρ τὴν ἰσχὺν καὶ τὸ πάχος οὐκ εὐαπόπνοα οὐδ᾿ εὐαφαίρετα· ζητοῦσι τὰ χρόνια — “because of their strength and their thickness they are not easily breathed away nor easily removed; for they seek what lasts.” τὸ νάρδινον is in that list. Part I quotes the other half of the same passage for the men’s florals.

And doctors prescribed fine scent against disease. Galen’s interest in these perfumes is therapeutic — they are “useful for such conditions of the stomach” — and the wider medical culture treated strong fragrance as a prophylactic. Herodian, describing the plague under Commodus, has the doctors ordering Romans to fill nose and ears with the most fragrant perfume on the theory that the scent occupies the passages first and blocks the corrupting air.

The earliest witness in the dossier and the one that explains the rest. Part I quotes the men’s half of this same sentence; the women’s half contains both τὸ νάρδινον and τὸ αἰγύπτιον — the Egyptian, which the lexical tradition of the opening section equates with the Mendesian.

Three cruxes

1. πιστική — the untranslated word. Both Gospels qualify the nard with πιστικῆς, and no one knows what it means. The candidates have been in play since antiquity: from πιστός, “trustworthy” and so genuine, unadulterated — which would make it a grade-word answering exactly to Pliny’s nine counterfeits; or from πίνω/potable, so liquid; or a place-name; or a corruption. The dossier takes no side. What deserves recording is that the word sits in the same semantic field the trade sources occupy: whatever else πιστική is doing, a first-century reader met it in a market where nine plants were sold as nard.

2. The thickness — five witnesses, and a reversal of judgement. This is the best-attested physical property of any perfume in the corpus, and the sources disagree about whether it is a virtue:

WitnessOn the consistencyVerdict
Theophrastus, De od. 42–43 (4th c. BCE)τὸ πάχος keeps the scent from being “breathed away”A virtue — the reason women choose it
Dioscorides, MM 1.62 (1st c. CE)“fluid and not gluey, unless it has resin in it”Neutral — a grade marker
Pliny, NH 13 (1st c. CE)unguentorum hoc crassissimum, of the neighbouring cinnamon perfumeNeutral — a class marker
Galen, Kühn 12.604 (2nd c. CE)παχύτερα ἢ ὥστε ἐγχεῖσθαι — must be cut with nard perfumeA nuisance — it will not pour into an ear

The same property, valued oppositely by a botanist and a clinician five centuries apart, and finally engineered by the workshop. That is a rare thing to be able to say about an ancient product.

3. κάρυα βασιλικά — the royal nut, unidentified. Oribasius’ colourant is the burnt shells of κάρυα βασιλικά. The dossier renders this literally as “royal nut” and leaves the plant open: the phrase is a market or grade name, and the project does not impose a modern species on it — the same discipline Part II applies to ἀρναβώ and καρπήσιον. Public-facing texts sometimes gloss it “hazelnut” or “walnut”; both are conventions, not findings. What the recipe does establish, whatever the nut, is the function: burnt shell, ground in a mortar, used to darken the finished perfume. A second unidentified nut, κάρυα ἰνδικά (“Indian nuts”), is weighed into the salka oil above and is equally open.

The modern reconstruction

The version presented in the Alchemies of Scent programme is an interpretation inspired by Pliny’s description — the seven-ingredient formula of NH 13.15 above — and was created by the Berlin olfactory artist Klara Ravat. As with the House of Mendes reconstructions of Part I, it is an evidence-based experimental interpretation rather than a claim to reproduce one ancient bottle; the open questions in this Part (which nard, which leaf, which nut, how thick) are precisely the variables such a reconstruction has to decide.
