Part VI — The nard family: foliatum, spicatum, nardinum

What this is. A testimonia dossier for the three names the ancient market gave the nard perfumes — νάρδινον (nardinon), the Greek trade name; φουλιᾶτον / foliatum, the perfume made with the leaf; and σπικᾶτον / spicatum, the one made with the spike. It belongs beside Parts I–II because the Mendesian’s own lexical tradition runs through it: Erotian reports the Hippocratic “Egyptian perfume” as “called by some Malabathrinon, by others the Mendesian” (see the opening section), and malabathron-leaf is precisely what separates foliatum from plain nardinon.

Every Greek and Latin word quoted below was copied from the project’s verified witness files, never typed from memory; the provenance of each is stated in the closing note. Identifications stay at the level of the ancient lexicon — no modern species is imposed on νάρδος.

Three names, one family

Name Greek / Latin What distinguishes it Key witness
Nardinon νάρδινον; nardinum The base trade name: an oil charged with nard and its companions. Made with the malabathron leaf or without it — the fork that generates the other two names. Dioscorides MM 1.62
Foliatum φουλιᾶτον; foliatum The leaf perfume — Latin folium = μαλάβαθρον. Pliny treats the word as simply another name for nardinum; Aëtius transmits it as its own recipe, three times over. Pliny NH 13.15; Aëtius XVI
Spicatum σπικᾶτον; spicatum The spike perfume — Latin spica = ναρδόσταχυς, the nard spike. Named only in the Roman-period clinical sources; no independent recipe survives. Galen, Kühn 12.429, 12.604

The names are Roman, and Galen says so. Both foliatum and spicatum enter Greek as loanwords wearing Latin dress, and Galen flags the borrowing every time he uses them — “what they call at Rome phouliaton,” “in the tongue of the Romans,” “the one called in Latin.” At Method of Medicine 8.5 he goes further and derives the things from the words: these preparations “seem to have been invented by the luxury at Rome, and to have their appellations from it.” That is a rare thing in the perfume record — a contemporary naming the market that produced a product.

The recipe: Dioscorides and Pliny agree

The two independent first-century witnesses give the same formula, ingredient for ingredient, in two languages. Dioscorides names the fork that Pliny’s double title (nardinum sive foliatum) records:

νάρδινον δὲ μύρον ποικίλως σκευάζεται διὰ τοῦ μαλαβαθρίνου φύλλου καὶ δίχα τούτου. ὡς τὸ πολὺ δὲ μείγνυται τῷ βαλανίνῳ ἐλαίῳ ἢ ὀμφακίνῳ, πρὸς μὲν τὴν στύψιν τοῦ ἐλαίου σχοῖνος, πρὸς δὲ τὴν εὐωδίαν βάλλεται κόστος, ἄμωμον, νάρδος, σμύρνα, βάλσαμον.

Nardinon perfume is prepared in various ways, with the malabathron leaf and without it. For the most part it is mixed with balanos oil or omphacine; for the astringing of the oil, aromatic rush; for the fragrance are thrown in costus, amomum, nard, myrrh, balsam.

Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.62, ed. M. Wellmann, p. 56.19–57.2 · urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0656.tlg001.1st1K-grc1:1.62

Seven for seven, plus the leaf. The convergence is not an editor’s harmonization: Wellmann’s own apparatus to this chapter glosses the Greek fork with the Latin name — conficitur cum folio malabatri et sine isto miscetur (foliatum a Pl. l. s. appellatur), “it is made with malabathron leaf and mixed without it (called foliatum by Pliny in the passage cited).” The equation was printed in the critical edition before it was a claim of ours.

And a simple version. Dioscorides closes with a plain grade — σκευάζεται δὲ καὶ λιτὴ δι᾿ ὀμφακίνου ἐλαίου καὶ σχοίνου καὶ καλάμου καὶ κόστου καὶ νάρδου: “a simple kind is also prepared, from omphacine oil and rush and reed and costus and nard” — no leaf, no myrrh, no balsam. The same two-tier structure as the Mendesian and susinum of Part I: a costly compound and a cheap one under one name.

Testimonia, alphabetical by author

One entry per witness, alphabetically by ancient author — the biblical books take their place in the same sequence rather than standing apart. Greek and Latin are quoted from the verified witnesses; translations are the project’s own.

Aëtius of Amida, Libri medicinales XVI

  • recipe · three foliatum formulae in sequence
  • olfactory: direct

Aëtius is the only source that transmits foliatum as a recipe rather than a name, and he gives three, one after another. The first:

ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ. Ὀποβαλσάμου λίτ. α. στύρακος, ἀμώμου ἀνὰ γο γ. κασίας γο δ. κρίνα τὸν ἀριθμὸν κ. λέαινε ἅμα καὶ χρῶ. εἰ δὲ παχύτερον εἴη, ἔκλυε αὐτὸ μύρῳ σαλκᾶ.

Preparation of foliatum. Opobalsam, 1 litra; storax and amomum, 3 ounces each; cassia, 4 ounces; lilies, twenty in number. Grind together and use. And if it should be too thick, dissolve it with salka perfume.

The second, headed ἄλλη γραφὴ φουλιάτου (“another writing of foliatum”), is a wax-bodied compound: wax 1 litra, Cyzicene nard 3 litrai, Brutian pitch 5 oz, ladanum 1 oz, oily storax 3 oz, costus and cassia 2 oz each, cardamom and leaf 1 oz each, nard-spike 1 oz, saffron and Aminaean wine 1 oz each, opobalsam 3 oz. The third is credited to another compiler — ΕΚ ΤΩΝ ΟΡΕΙΒΑΣΙΟΥ ΦΟΥΛΙΑΤΟΥ ΣΚΕΥΑΣΙΑ, “from Oribasius: preparation of foliatum” — and returns the leaf to the centre: cassia 6 oz, leaf 3 oz, amomum and ladanum 1 oz each, pepper 40 grains, Troglodytic myrrh 1 oz, nard perfume 1 litra, pounded and sifted through the finest sieve.

Aëtius of Amida, Libri medicinales XVI (ed. Zervos), Phouliatou skeuasia and the two following · urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0718.tlg016

Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 15

  • report · appellation catalogue and comic quotation
  • olfactory: direct

In the catalogue of named appellations that Part I quotes for the Mendesian, nard perfume has its own city: νάρδινον δὲ τὸ ἐν Τάρσῳ — “nardinon, that made at Tarsus.” The same book classes it among the perfumes made from roots — ἀπὸ δὲ ῥιζῶν τό τ᾿ ἴρινον, καὶ τὸ νάρδινον, καὶ τὸ ἀμαράκινον ἐκ τοῦ κόστου — and preserves Menander joking about it: ἡδὺ τὸ μύρον, παιδίον. Β. ἡδύ· πῶς γὰρ οὔ; νάρδινον, “Sweet perfume, boy. — Sweet; how not? It’s nardinon.”

Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 15 (688e, and the root-perfume and Menander notices) · urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0008.tlg001

Dioscorides, De materia medica 1.62

  • recipe · the base nardinon, compound and simple
  • olfactory: direct

Quoted in full above. Two further clauses belong to the dossier. The quality test is a smell test: ἐστι δὲ χρηστὴ ἡ λεπτή, μὴ δριμεῖα, ἔχουσα τὴν εὐωδίαν τῆς ξηρᾶς νάρδου ἢ ἀμώμου — “the good kind is the light one, not pungent, having the fragrance of dry nard or of amomum.” And the consistency clause anticipates the complaint Galen and Aëtius both make: ὑγρὰ δέ ἐστι καὶ οὐ γλοιώδης, ἐὰν μὴ ῥητίνην ἔχῃ — “it is fluid and not gluey, unless it has resin in it.”

Quoted in full above. Pliny follows the formula immediately with the trade’s dark side: in hoc genere conveniet meminisse herbarum, quae nardum Indicum imitarentur, species VIIII a nobis esse dictas: tanta materia adulterandi est — “in this class it will be well to recall the plants that imitate Indian nard, nine species of which we have spoken: so great is the material for adulteration.” Nine counterfeits for one aromatic; compare the balsam fraud-tests of Part I, where the cheap perfume is itself the vehicle of the fraud.

Song of Songs 1:12; 4:13–14

  • report · nard in the love poetry of the Hebrew Bible
  • Greek not quoted — see the note

Nard appears twice in the Song: at the banquet, where the king reclines and the speaker’s nard “gives forth its fragrance” (1:12), and in the catalogue of the enclosed garden, where nard stands at the head of a list running through saffron, aromatic reed and cinnamon to myrrh and aloes (4:13–14). That list is the same shelf as the recipes above — νάρδος, κρόκος, κάλαμος, cinnamon, myrrh — which is why the passage belongs here and not merely as decoration.

Provenance note. The project corpus holds no Septuagint witness, so no Greek is quoted for this entry and the references are given by chapter and verse only. It should be quoted from a named edition before it is treated as evidence on the same footing as the entries above. See the closing note.

Two cruxes

1. πιστική — the untranslated word. Both Gospels qualify the nard with πιστικῆς, and no one knows what it means. The candidates have been in play since antiquity: from πιστός, “trustworthy” and so genuine, unadulterated — which would make it a grade-word answering exactly to Pliny’s nine counterfeits; or from πίνω/potable, so liquid; or a place-name; or a corruption. The dossier takes no side. What deserves recording is that the word sits in the same semantic field the trade sources occupy: whatever else πιστική is doing, a first-century reader met it in a market where nine plants were sold as nard.

2. The thickness, attested three times. Dioscorides says nardinon is fluid “unless it has resin in it”; Galen says spicatum and foliatum are “thicker than can be poured” and must be cut with nard perfume; Aëtius’ first foliatum recipe ends “if it should be too thick, dissolve it with salka perfume.” A physical property of a named commercial product, independently attested in a pharmacologist, a clinician and a recipe — and, in the recipe, the workshop’s own remedy for it. Pliny’s adjacent verdict on the cinnamon perfume, unguentorum hoc crassissimum (“this is the thickest of the unguents”), puts the same register in Latin.
