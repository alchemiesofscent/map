Part V — The queens’ fragments

What this is. A structured evidence dossier of every ancient passage so far collected that connects a named Ptolemaic queen with perfume, smell, cosmetics, drugs, or aromatic commodities — sixteen records: six recipes and ten reports, drawn from the project’s working repository perfume-tables/research/ptolemaic-queens-fragments (v0.1, 15 August 2026). Every record carries a verified edition, page, and line or verse locator. It is the documentary floor under the “Cleopatra question” of the opening section: no ancient source records Cleopatra’s personal perfume, but a rich double tradition survives — recipes transmitted under a Cleopatra’s name in the medical compilers, and reports of perfume around the court: patronage, spectacle, poetry, and aromatic revenue.

The classification is deliberately conservative. Recipe means a procedural formulation with ingredients or operations; report covers narrative, testimony, poetry, economics, and spectacle. Olfactory relevance is graded separately (direct, indirect, contextual, none), and attribution is not inflated: “Cleopatra’s Cosmetic” names the authority a later medical source transmits — it does not by itself prove authorship by Cleopatra VII, and the records say so.

The perfumes and aromatics the fragments record

Two aromatics anchor the set, from opposite ends of the evidence. Spikenard (nard) appears both as a weighed ingredient — one ounce of ναρδόσταχυς in the one queen-attributed perfume recipe, the fragrant cleanser below — and as court imagery, the “flowering nard” woven into the garlands of Cleopatra’s banquet in Lucan. Cinnamon appears only in the second register: poured over the diners’ hair, praised precisely for having “not yet lost the breath of its foreign land” — freshness of import as the mark of luxury, the same long-distance trade the provenance map plots for the recipes of Parts I–II.

The overlap with the House of Mendes shelf is the point: spikenard, cassia, rush flower, Troglodytic myrrh, and myrobalan in the cleanser are the same materials, and in two cases the same named grades, as the perfume recipes of Part I — one supply chain serving perfumery, medicine, and the royal toilette alike.

The one queen-attributed perfume recipe: the fragrant cleanser

The clearest queen-attributed perfume formulation in the medical corpus is not an oil perfume at all but a powdered smegma, a body cleanser, transmitted by Aëtius of Amida under the heading “Another costly, fragrant cleanser of Queen Cleopatra” — the only record in the set whose heading says basilissa, queen. Aëtius is a sixth-century compiler and gives no dynastic number; the attribution is recorded at low confidence, traditional rather than proven.

  • Aëtius of Amida, Libri medicinales 8.6
  • olfactory: direct
  • recipe · fragrant body cleanser

Ἄλλο σμῆγμα Κλεοπάτρας βασιλίσσης πολυτελὲς εὐῶδες. κόστου σμύρνης τρωγλίτιδος ἴρεως ναρδοστάχυος ἀμώμου φύλλου κασσίας σχοίνου ἄνθους ἀνὰ Γο αʹ μυροβαλάνου λίτρας δʹ νίτρου ἀφροῦ λίτρας βʹ κόψας σήσας χρῶ· ποιεῖ εἰς ὅλον τὸ σῶμα.

Another costly, fragrant cleanser of Queen Cleopatra. Of costus, Troglodytic myrrh, iris, spikenard, amomum leaf, cassia, and rush flower, one ounce each; of myrobalan, four litrai; of natron foam, two litrai. Pound, sift, and use. It is suitable for the whole body.

Aëtius of Amida, Libri medicinales 8.6.31–34, ed. A. Olivieri, CMG VIII/2 (Berlin 1950), p. 408.18–21 · TLG 0718:001

The proportions tell the workshop story: seven aromatics at one ounce each (~27 g) carried in four litrai (~1.3 kg) of powdered myrobalan with two litrai of natron foam — the same economy as the perfumes of Part III, where the base is nearly free and the fortune is in the aromatics. At Diocletian’s edict prices the ounce of Troglodytic myrrh alone is a day’s skilled wages.

Nard and cinnamon at Cleopatra’s table

Lucan stages Caesar at Cleopatra’s Alexandrian banquet; the passage is epic representation, not inventory, but its aromatics are chosen with a merchant’s eye:

  • Lucan, Bellum civile 10.164–168
  • olfactory: direct
  • report · perfumed banquet

accipiunt sertas nardo florente coronas / et numquam fugiente rosa, multumque madenti / infudere comae quod nondum euanuit aura / cinnamon externa nec perdidit aera terrae, / aduectumque recens uicinae messis amomon.

They receive garlands woven with flowering nard and with a rose that never fades. Over their drenched hair they pour much cinnamon that has not yet lost the breath of its foreign land, and amomum newly brought from the neighboring harvest.

Lucan, Bellum civile 10.164–168, ed. A. E. Housman (Oxford 1926), p. 308 · PHI LAT0917

Every clause is a supply-chain boast: nard still flowering, cinnamon that has not yet gone stale in transit from its foreign land, amomum from a neighboring harvest. Lucan’s luxury is measured in freshness across distance — precisely the corridors the interactive map draws as conventional routes, and the reason Dioscorides’ quality tests in Part I keep insisting on fresh material.

Patronage, spectacle, poetry, economy

Patronage. The dossier’s most direct patronage evidence is the Apollonius “Mys” fragment already quoted in the opening section, here as a structured record: Alexandria’s perfumes flourished “because of wealth and because of the devotion (σπουδή) of Arsinoe and Berenice,” and Cyrene’s best rose perfume is dated by the lifetime of “Berenice the Great” (Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 15.38, 688e–689b, quoting Apollonius of Tyre). Royal women periodize perfume production; that is patronage functioning as chronology.

Spectacle. At the Cydnus, Plutarch’s one sensory clause does the work of a set-piece: “extraordinary scents from the great quantity of incense filled the riverbanks” (Plutarch, Life of Antony 26.3) — engineered smell extending the royal performance beyond the barge into the landscape. Behind it stands the dynasty’s founding template: Ptolemy II’s great procession with its 350 gold incense-burners and 800 wagons of aromatics, the Bereniceion’s doorway dressed within the same parade (Athenaeus, Deipnosophistae 5.34, 203a–b, quoting Callixenus of Rhodes) — a queen’s cult monument embedded in a spectacle saturated with aromatic wealth.

Poetry. Callimachus makes a Berenice the fourth Grace, “just fashioned and still wet with perfumes” (μύροισι νοτεῖ, Epigram 51 Pfeiffer = Anthologia Palatina 5.146): the scented royal body as divine beauty. Florus closes the arc at the other end of the dynasty — Cleopatra in her grandest finery, laid beside Antony “on a couch steeped in perfumes” (Florus, Epitome 2.21.11): fragrance as royal self-presentation, kept up into death.

Economy. Josephus records the hardest-headed item: after Antony’s territorial grants, Herod leased back from Cleopatra the revenues around Jericho — the country that “produces balsam, the most valuable product there” (Josephus, Antiquitates Judaicae 15.96). An aromatic monopoly as royal income stream: the same balsam whose fraud-detection chemistry and prices Part I reports from Pliny.

The boundary cases. The set deliberately keeps its comparators: Galen’s note that Criton collected “all the drugs of Heracleides and Cleopatra” (bibliography, not perfume); Plutarch’s toxicology narrative and Pliny’s poisoned-crown anecdote (drug lore that later tradition blurs into perfume lore); and the non-olfactory medical recipes under Cleopatra’s name — alopecia plaster, falling-hair pastille, scalp washes. In the strongest of them the perfume connection runs backwards: the hair-growth salve of bear fat and cedar oil treats smell as a defect and adds wine “to blunt the smell.” The queens’ dossier separates what the sources separate: perfume culture, cosmetic craft, and drug lore.
