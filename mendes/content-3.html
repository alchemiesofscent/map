In: the base of the dark Mendesian (all three witnesses) and of simple susinum (Dsc. MM 1.52.5). Identification: genuinely open — the best open question in the piece. Two candidates, Balanites aegyptiaca (desert date) and Moringa (ben-nut), are both attested archaeologically in Egyptian oil production, and no ancient description decides between them. Our laboratory runs both. The ancient descriptions: Dioscorides 4.157 — fruit of a tree "resembling the tamarisk," like a hazelnut; its inside, pressed, gives out a liquid "as bitter almonds do, which they use in costly perfumes in place of oil"; the best is "fresh, full, white, and easily peeled." Pliny NH 12.100–103 grades the market by shell color region by region (best of all the Petraean: black shell, white kernel) and gives the trade secret: "perfumers press only the husks; physicians the kernels too." Price: 2 denarii a pound in Pliny (≈ $400 at the day's-wage anchor) — while myrrh commanded hundreds of denarii: the base was nearly free; the aromatics were the fortune. Looks like: Balanites — a date-like drupe, 2.5–4 cm, yellow-brown, fibrous flesh over a hard ridged stone; Moringa — long ribbed pods to 30+ cm with pale three-winged seeds. For the shoot: both candidates side by side, labeled; if possible husks and kernels separated — the perfumer's half and the physician's half of the same nut. Sourcing: both candidate materials Prague stock. Moringa seed and oil are commercially easy; Balanites fruit is the long-lead item — start it first.

βαλσάμου καρπός / καρποβάλσαμον (karpobalsamon) — balsam seed

In: metopion (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125). Identification: the fruit of the balsam of Judaea (conventionally Commiphora gileadensis), the most valuable liquid-producing shrub in the Roman world (Pliny NH 12.111–123). Theophrastus (HP 9.6) knew it three centuries earlier as a plant existing in exactly two cultivated gardens in the Jordan valley — one of about twenty plethra, one much smaller, "nowhere any wild balsam" — a tree the size of a large pomegranate with a whitish rue-like evergreen leaf, incised with iron nails, not knives, in the stifling heat, yielding one shell-measure per man per day; the unmixed resin sold "for twice its weight in silver" and was adulterated before it ever reached Greece. And the detail that matters for this ingredient: the fruit, terebinth-like in size, shape and colour, he calls even more fragrant than the resin (9.6.1) — the perfumers' karpobalsamon was not a substitute for the precious opobalsam but a prized aromatic in its own right. Looks like: small reddish-brown ovoid capsules, a few millimetres, on the dried twig. Sourcing: the hardest item on the list — the plant survives in cultivation (Arabian peninsula; conservation collections in Israel and Jordan). Talk to us before attempting to source; we can advise and may be able to arrange study material.

ἔλαιον (elaion) — olive oil

In: susinum stage 1 (Dsc. MM 1.52); Paul's susinum. Sourcing: trivial; for authenticity, a green early-harvest oil (see omphacine, next).

ἔλαιον ὀμφάκινον (elaion omphakinon) — omphacine oil, "unripe-fruit oil"

In: metopion (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125). Identification: the fine, astringent grade pressed from unripe olives — the prestige perfume base (so also in the amarakinon, Aët. 1.128). Looks like: a distinctly green, slightly cloudy oil. Sourcing: early-harvest ("agourelaio"-style) Greek oil is a fair modern proxy; we press our own — Prague stock.

κάλαμος ἀρωματικός (kalamos arōmatikos) — aromatic reed

In: metopion; susinum stage 1 (charged oil); Paul's susinum at 5 litrai. Identification: conventionally sweet flag (Acorus calamus) rhizome, but "reed" identifications in the aromatics trade are debated; we flag, not settle. Theophrastus (HP 9.7.1) grows it beside the aromatic rush in the Lebanon valley by the lake (see the σχοῖνος entry for his full description): scentless green, fragrant only dried, indistinguishable by eye from common reed — the smell, not the look, is the identity. Looks like: finger-thick dried rhizome sections, tan and wrinkled outside, pale spongy pith inside; warm, sweet-spicy smell. Sourcing: Prague stock (this is one of the four we routinely ship).

καρδάμωμον (kardamōmon) — cardamom

In: metopion (a marker of quality — the approved kind "shows the cardamom"); susinum, in every stage and at the heart of the finishing-triad crux; Paul's susinum at 3 litrai. Identification: an eastern spice of the cardamom/amomum group; whether ancient καρδάμωμον is the modern true cardamom (Elettaria) or a related capsule is not fully settled. Already Theophrastus (HP 9.7.2) could not pin its origin: "As for cardamom and amomum, some say they come from Media, others say from India" — the supply chain was obscure from the start. Ancient handling note: used "cleaned, the pods without the core" — i.e. the husked seed. Looks like: pale green-to-straw three-sided capsules; split one to show the dark resinous seeds. Sourcing: any good spice merchant (whole green pods).

καρπήσιον (karpēsion) — karpesion

In: Paul's susinum, third permitted substitute for cinnamon (7.20.8). Identification: a bark/twig aromatic known to Galen (who compares it to valerian-family drugs); no secure modern identity. For the shoot: skip, or caption alongside arnabo as the unidentifiables.

κασσία, κασσία σύριγξ (kassia, kassia syrinx) — cassia; "quill cassia"

In: the dark Mendesian in all three witnesses (Paul: 3 oz of the quill grade); cassia at double weight is the perfumers' cinnamon substitute (Paul 7.20.8; Galen). Identification: cinnamon-bark family (cassia group); σῦριγξ, "pipe," is not a plant but a market grade — Galen (De antidotis, Kühn XIV.73) walks the whole hierarchy: gizi (the best, "nearly identical to cinnamon and often sold as it"), moto, arebo, daphnitis, and at the bottom syrinx, "the cheapest of all: its outer bark … is strong in smell and taste, but its inner part is extremely weak." The Mendesian, by Paul's letter, uses the cheap grade — a perfume built on patient time rather than costly bark. Theophrastus (HP 9.5.3) describes the plant and a startling processing step: rods "thicker [than cinnamon], very fibrous," impossible to strip clean — so the cut two-finger lengths are sewn up in fresh-flayed hide, and the worms that breed there eat away the wood but do not touch the bark, "because of the bitterness and pungency of its smell." Quality control by worm. Looks like: thick single-layer rolled bark tubes, coarser and darker than true-cinnamon quills. For the camera: the rolled quill form is the name — shoot it as tubes. Sourcing: any good spice merchant (sold as cassia or "Chinese cinnamon" quills).

κινάμωμον (kinamōmon) — cinnamon

In: the Mendesian as the disputed fifth ingredient (Dsc.: added "uselessly"; Aët.: sprinkled in by "some" after the astringing; Paul: canonical at 3 oz — watch the recipe harden across six centuries); susinum finishing triad (by editorial conjecture — see the crux) and Paul's susinum at 3 oz. Identification: the ancient cinnamon/cassia distinction does not map neatly onto the modern C. verum / C. cassia split; both ancient terms sit in the cinnamon-bark family. Galen reports the belief that the best cassia turns into true cinnamon at the top of the same bush. Theophrastus (HP 9.5.1–2) already knows the grade logic: a shrub the size of a chaste-tree, cut and divided into five grades by distance from the shoot-tip — best at the tip, worst at the root, "for the bark is what is useful, not the wood." He also transmits, and flatly rejects, the harvest legend: cinnamon growing in ravines full of deadly snakes, the gatherers dividing the crop into three and leaving the Sun's portion, which bursts into flame as they depart — "this one," he says, "is genuinely a myth" (οὗτος μὲν οὖν τῷ ὄντι μῦθος). Looks like: tight multi-layered pale-brown quills (modern "Ceylon" type) — visibly finer than the cassia tubes; photograph the two side by side. Sourcing: any good spice merchant.

κόμμι (kommi) — gum

In: susinum jar-coating (with myrrh, saffron, honey; Dsc. MM 1.52.4). Identification: gum arabic (acacia gum), the Egyptian gum par excellence — Theophrastus knows the Egyptian acacia as a gum-source and states the principle that fits its job here: gum, being fat-free, is scentless (HP 9.1.2–3) — a binder and sealant, never an aromatic. Looks like: glassy amber-to-pale nodules. Sourcing: trivial (food-grade acacia gum).

κρίνον / λείριον (krinon / leirion) — lily

In: susinum is the lily perfume: 1,000 stripped blossoms per cycle (Dsc. MM 1.52), petals at 10 litrai (Paul 7.20.8). Identification: conventionally the Madonna lily (Lilium candidum), the white lily of the eastern Mediterranean. Looks like: the shoot's centrepiece — white trumpet blossoms stripped from the stem, lying in a thin bed in a broad shallow basin under sheeting oil; hands glossed with honey. Sourcing: the seasonal risk item. Madonna lilies flower May–July and are largely out of season by late August; commercial white lilies (L. longiflorum) are the year-round stand-in but are a different species — confirm availability and decide the honesty policy for captions first.

κρόκος (krokos) — saffron

In: susinum finish, 10 drachms per batch, and the jar-coating (Dsc. MM 1.52.4); Paul's susinum specifies Cilician saffron, 5 oz. Identification: secure (Crocus sativus stigmas). The named ancient grades are Corycian and Cilician — the same Anatolian coast; "fresh" is always emphasized. In Diocletian's price edict Arabian saffron stands at 2,000 denarii/lb (≈ $8,000 — see Part III), the most expensive aromatic on the list. Looks like: deep-red dried stigmas ("threads") with orange styles. Sourcing: any good spice merchant; buy whole threads, never powder.

μέλι (meli) — honey

In: metopion (an ingredient proper — Attic honey, 3 litrai in Paul's weighed recipe); susinum as workshop material — hands smeared with it, vessels coated with it. Looks like: for the camera, a pale runny honey glossing hands and bowl-rims reads best. Sourcing: trivial.

οἶνος (oinos) — wine

In: metopion (fragrant wine, 4 xestai, "for soaking the dry ingredients," Paul 7.20.16); susinum stage 1 ("fragrant wine" for kneading the myrrh); Paul's susinum (steeping myrrh and reed, 5 days). Sourcing: trivial; an amber-coloured wine photographs closer to the ancient product than a modern pale white.

ῥητίνη / τερεβινθίνη (rhētinē / terebinthinē) — resin; terebinth resin

In: the Mendesian (Paul: 1 litra terebinth, melted in after the sixty days); metopion (Pliny specifies the resin as terebinth). Identification: secure — the resin of the terebinth (Pistacia terebinthus and relatives). Theophrastus (HP 9.2) puts terebinth at the head of the resin hierarchy — the most fragrant and the lightest, above fir and pine. But note how it was used: Dioscorides says resin went into unguents always processed — combined "neither to preserve them nor to make them more pleasurable, but for colour and viscosity," the terebinth resin boiled down until odourless before mixing (MM 1.58, on the amarakinon). The perfume's resin is body, not scent — worth a caption beside the raw fragrant lumps. Looks like: soft, translucent pale-gold to greenish lumps, faintly turpentine-fresh in smell; melts glossy. Sourcing: Prague stock (routinely shipped).

σμύρνα (smyrna) — myrrh

In: all three perfumes — the Mendesian (3 oz), metopion (a marker of the approved grade), susinum (stage 1, finish, and jar-coating). Identification: secure at genus level for the Greek word (Commiphora gum-resin). The grades the ancients prized were the "fatty" and "thickest," Troglodytic and Arabian; edict price 400 denarii/lb (≈ $1,600 at the edict's $4/den. anchor — see Part III). An Egyptian caution for captions: the Egyptian word conventionally translated "myrrh," ꜥnt.w (𓂝𓈖𓏏𓅱, antu), is a category, not a species — the Edfu temple antu-list gives fourteen varieties, and chemical analyses of jars labeled antu have found animal fat, bitumen and plant resins without myrrh compounds. Greek σμύρνα is the secure term; "the Egyptians' myrrh" is not a safe caption. Theophrastus (HP 9.4) puts the tree in Arabia "around Saba and Hadramyta": small and shrubby, "with a hard trunk twisted along the ground, thicker than shin-width," thorny, terebinth-like, "with a leaf resembling the elm, except curly and thorny at the tip"; the resin is cut under the Dog Star in the hottest days, falling onto woven palm-mats (the mat-caught tears cleaner than the ground-fallen — a grading detail made for the camera), and past-prime trees are the more fragrant. He splits the grades into στακτή, the free-running liquid myrrh (cf. the Egyptian mesha-ib entry on the Egyptian shelf below), and πλαστή, the "moulded," and describes the Sabaean honour-market: heaps left unguarded at the temple of the Sun, each with a price-tablet, the god taking a third. Looks like: irregular red-brown to amber tears with a pale bloom where fractured; oily sheen on fresh break. Sourcing: Prague stock (routinely shipped); commercial myrrh varies wildly in grade — ours matches the chemistry runs.

σχοῖνος (schoinos) — aromatic rush

In: metopion (Paul weighs the rush-flower, σχοίνου ἄνθους, at 1 litra). Identification: the aromatic rush ("camel grass," conventionally Cymbopogon schoenanthus). Theophrastus (HP 9.7.1) grows it with the aromatic reed in one place: "when one crosses over the Lebanon range … in that little valley," beside a large lake "in the dried-out marsh," over an area of more than thirty stadia — and "they have no scent when green, only when dried, and in appearance they differ in no way from ordinary reeds. But as one enters the area, the smell strikes at once." He also debunks the tale that ships offshore could smell it — the site is 150+ stadia from the sea. Looks like: a bundle of dried grass-like stems and leaf, hay-gold, lemony-warm when crushed. For the shoot: the rush bundle, dried — the drying is the point. Sourcing: specialist herb suppliers (Middle Eastern markets, as idhkhir/lemongrass relatives) or via Prague.

ὕδωρ ὄμβριον (hydōr ombrion) — rainwater

In: susinum (soaking the cardamom, Dsc. MM 1.52.1). The specification is deliberate: rainwater is soft, mineral-free. Sourcing: the sky; deionized water is the lab equivalent.

χαλβάνη (chalbanē) — galbanum

In: metopion — the eponym: the perfume is named "because of the admixture of galbanum, for the tree from which galbanum comes is called metopon" (Dsc. MM 1.59.1). Note the quality test runs against it: the approved metopion shows cardamom and myrrh more than the galbanum. Identification: secure at genus level (gum-resin of Ferula species). Theophrastus knows it as the sap of the plant called panakes, "around Syria," cut at wheat-harvest time (HP 9.9.2) — and rates it aromatically with a wrinkled nose: "heavier and more drug-like" than Syria's outstanding scents (HP 9.7.2). Metopion's name-ingredient was, by the first botanist's judgment, the least perfumey thing in it — which is exactly what Dioscorides' quality test implies too. Looks like: sticky amber-to-olive lumps, often with stem fragments embedded; intensely green, sharp smell. Sourcing: essential-oil and incense trade (Iranian galbanum resin); or via Prague.

The Egyptian shelf — the same materials in hieroglyphs

The Greek recipes above are the Mediterranean face of a much older Egyptian craft, and its own records survive — most spectacularly in the jz (𓇋𓊃), the "laboratory" of the temple of Edfu: a room off the inner hall whose walls are covered in recipes, ingredient lists and ritual instructions for the sacred ointments. A few items from that shelf, for captions and for the visual pairing of Greek jar and temple wall:

Egyptian Hieroglyphs What it is
ꜥnt.w (antu) 𓂝𓈖𓏏𓅱 The word conventionally translated "myrrh" — actually a category of sacred oleo-resins: the Edfu antu-list gives fourteen varieties, by color, texture and divine origin (see the σμύρνα entry above)
zšn (seshen) — Lotus/lily — the probable ultimate source of the trade-name σούσινον itself (see §3)
nnjb (nenib) 𓈖𓈖𓇋𓃀 Conventionally "styrax" since an 1881 sound-match with Hebrew and Arabic; the Edfu texts describe not a resin but woods — black, red, white — classified by scent, color, season and divinity
hedju 𓌉𓌉𓌉𓆰𓏥 Not a species but a grade label ("first-quality resin") recurring across different antu-list entries — the Egyptian counterpart of Galen's cassia-grade hierarchy in §1
ḫsḫs (kheskhes) 𓐍𓋴𓐍𓋴 A wood banned from sacred ointments: reddish, "bad in all respects," its scent from the stench of Seth — rejection by theology, not by quality control
tꜢ-nṯr 𓇾𓊹 "The Divine Land" — the stated origin of sacred aromatics (with Punt); in the inscriptions it behaves like theology, not geography: it says why the material matters, not where it grew

Two Edfu items earn a longer caption. Antu-list entry 8 (mesha-ib, 𓅓𓂝𓈚𓎺𓏏): a red, soft, sweet-smelling resin from "Kemati-land," with the instruction to press it through a cloth bag — a quarter comes out as liquid. That is the Greek στακτή (staktē), the pressed liquid myrrh, described from the Egyptian side; long dismissed as impossible without a solvent, the process has now been shown experimentally to work with fresh Commiphora myrrh (and not with Pistacia resins) — the Egyptian and Greek texts describing one real technique from two sides. And the laboratory's own masterworks put our sixty-day Mendesian in perspective: tisheps, Hathor's perfume, took 303 days (nedjem-juice base, first-quality antu, nenib ground and triple-sifted, acorus — cf. the κάλαμος/sweet flag of the Greek shelf — steeped, sealed in alabaster, rested in staged masses; Edfu II 229–230), and medjet, the one fat-based ointment, took two years and began with a temple-raised bull (Edfu II 227–228). Cold patience as technique is an Egyptian inheritance.

Part III — What perfume cost, and who kept the books

USD equivalents are wage-anchored and indicative only (a skilled laborer's day ≈ $200): the classical Athenian drachma and the 1st-century Roman denarius are each roughly one day's pay (≈ $200); the Diocletian-edict denarius of 301 CE is a heavily inflated unit converted at $4 (the edict's own skilled day = 50 den.). Cross-era comparisons are order-of-magnitude only.

  • Athens, from comedy: a kotylē (about a quarter-litre) of fine perfume at five minae (Hipparchus) or ten (Menander) — 500–1,000 drachmas, roughly $100,000–200,000 for under a litre; stakte haggled over at two minae (≈ $40,000). Inflated for laughs, but the pricing unit is real.
  • Menander's party math: a sacrificial sheep for the gods, 10 drachmas (≈ $2,000); flute-girls, perfume, wine, eels, cheese and honey for the guests, "nearly a talent" — 6,000 drachmas, call it $1.2 million.
  • A real institutional budget: the Peripatos under Lycon collected nine obols a head per month (1½ drachmas, ≈ $300) — "not even enough for the perfuming and the garlands." A start-up loan for a perfumery ran at 18%.
  • Rome (Pliny NH 13.20): unguents "exceed 400 denarii a pound" — over a year's daily wages, ≈ $80,000/lb — "at that price is another's pleasure bought — for the one who wears the scent does not smell it himself."
  • Raw beats compounded. In Diocletian's price edict (301 CE, at $4/den.): Arabian saffron 2,000 denarii/lb (≈ $8,000), Troglodytic myrrh 400 (≈ $1,600), cassia 120 (≈ $480) — but finished rose oil only 80 (≈ $320) and lily oil 100 (≈ $400; top of the perfumed-oil tier, with marjoram). The value was in the raw material, not the labor. (These are legal maxima in an inflating currency; the sworn price returns of the Oxyrhynchus perfume-sellers' guild — cassia at 500 den./lb in 312, frankincense inflating tenfold in fifteen years — are the better ground truth, though by then the anchor itself is dissolving.)
