    How to read the map

    Evidence classes

    • Ancient-attested claim. A Greek, Latin, or Egyptian source states this origin.
    • Inference or modern correction. The recipe is silent about provenance, or later evidence corrects the ancient claim.
    • Theological provenance. Divine-origin language, deliberately not pinned as literal geography.
    • Court record — report, not provenance. A queen-connected report from the Ptolemaic queens’ dossier that names a city: patronage, spectacle, or aromatic revenue. Off by default; switch on “Court records” in the layer rail. A report never becomes an ingredient-origin dot.

    Markers show the evidence class only. Use the perfume layers above the map to see which points belong to a recipe; each point also names its recipes when you select or hover it.

    Touch

    Drag to pan, pinch to zoom. Double-tap to zoom in a step; a two-finger tap zooms out. Double-tap and hold, then drag down or up, to zoom in or out continuously around the tapped point.

    Keyboard

    ← / → step through the selected ingredient’s places in source order; ↑ / ↓ move between ingredients; Esc closes panes and releases the map.

    Route caution

    Every sea, land, and Nile corridor on this map is a conventional reconstruction—not a route attested for any single ingredient.

    One ingredient at a time

    This map answers where the three recipes' materials came from, all claims at once. To walk the places attested for a single ingredient, in the order its source names them, select it — from the index below, the search, or a catalogue link — and step through its stops with the panel's arrows or the strip along the foot of the map.

    Sources and editions

    Ancient citations on the dots use book and chapter divisions. Page and line anchors remain in the verified local editions and structured recipe records.

    1. Dioscorides, De materia medica, ed. Wellmann: 1.6 (cardamom); 1.13–14 (cassia/cinnamon); 1.17–18 (schoinos/kalamos); 1.26 (saffron); 1.52 (Susinum); 1.59 (Metopion and Mendesian); 1.64 (myrrh); 1.71 (terebinth resin); 3.83–84 (galbanum and ammoniacum as distinct substances); 4.157 (balanos, without a provenance claim).
    2. Theophrastus, Historia plantarum 9.7.1–3: the Lebanon valley, Syria, Arabia, India, and Media provenance reports.
    3. Herodotus, Histories 3.107–111: the Arabian claim for cassia and cinnamon, retained here as an ancient claim rather than harmonized with later evidence.
    4. Pliny the Elder, Naturalis historia 12.50; 12.66–70; 12.82–98; 12.100–107; 12.111–126; 13.5–11: cardamom, myrrh grades, cinnamon/cassia, balanos grades, aromatic reed/rush, hammoniacum from the metopon tree near the oracle of Hammon, balsam, galbanum, and Egyptian perfume manufacture.
    5. Josephus, Bellum Judaicum 4.469: Jericho as a producer of opobalsam, alongside henna and myrobalanos.
    6. Paul of Aegina, Epitome medica 7.20.8, 7.20.16, 7.20.31, ed. Heiberg: Susinum, Metopion, and Mendesian recipe witnesses; 7.20.16 explicitly specifies Attic honey.
    7. Aëtius of Amida, Libri medicinales 1.125–126, ed. Olivieri: Metopion and Mendesian ingredient witnesses; no additional ingredient-origin claims were silently inferred from the recipe headings.
    8. Galen, De antidotis 1.14 (Kühn XIV 71–72): καρπήσιον most abundant and cheapest at Side in Pamphylia.
    9. Edfu II 204–208 and Wilde, Míčková, Pehal & Coughlin, “The Antu-List Reconsidered,” ZÄS 152.1 (2025), 117–135: “Things of the Land of god” and explicitly divine origins for temple aromatics.
    10. Modern correction register. Gilboa & Namdar, “On the Beginnings of South Asian Spice Trade,” Radiocarbon 57.2 (2015), 265–283; and Kew, Plants of the World Online. These correct the broad source-zone eastward without forcing Greek κἀσσία or κινάμωμον onto a single modern species.

    Claims not verified—and therefore not silently plotted as ancient origins

    • En Gedi as the balsam source. Pliny 12.111–123 gives Judaea and two royal gardens but does not name En Gedi; Josephus BJ 4.469 verifies Jericho. En Gedi is omitted.
    • Egyptian origin or pressing of ἔλαιον ὀμφάκινον. The sources place Metopion manufacture/invention in Egypt; they do not say the omphacine oil itself came from Egypt. Its workshop marker is dashed. Pliny 13.8 does separately attest bitter-almond oil expression in Egypt.
    • Local or Aegean origins for ordinary oil, fragrant wine, rainwater, fine salt, gum, and generic Susinum honey. The recipes name or use them but do not state a provenance. Dashed points mark working supply context only.
    • Local lily cultivation. Dioscorides 1.52.5 says the best finished Susinum is made in Phoenicia and Egypt; it does not state where the lilies themselves grew. Product and inferred flower-supply markers are distinguished.
    • ἀρναβώ (arnabō). Paul 7.20.8 transmits it as a Susinum substitute but provides no geographical origin; it remains unlocated in the index.
    • Exact coordinates for Pliny’s interior myrrh grade names (Atramitic, Gebbanitic, Ausaritis, Dianitis, Sambracena, Dusaritis, Mesalum). The names are retained under the Arabian claim, but not assigned invented point coordinates.
    • Which “Bosporus” Dioscorides 1.6 intends. The source’s toponym is plotted approximately at the Thracian Bosporus, where the strait meets the Sea of Marmara, and flagged in its note; the textual referent is not silently disambiguated.
    • A botanical identity for generic ῥητίνη, σύριγξ, καρπήσιον, or Pliny’s metopon. Conventional glosses are supplied for reading; the map does not force modern taxa.
