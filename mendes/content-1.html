    Prepared for: Allyson (National Geographic)
    From: Alchemies of Scent, Institute of Philosophy, Czech Academy of Sciences / IOCB Prague

    All translations are ours. Ancient plant names are given in Greek with transliteration and a conventional gloss; where the botanical identification is uncertain, we say so.     Executive summary for Allyson

    The story in one paragraph

    No ancient source records Cleopatra's personal perfume. The historically secure story is richer: ancient writers identify Mendesian and Metopion as leading Egyptian perfumes, while another tradition calls the lily perfume Susinum the white Mendesian. Together they can be presented as a house of Mendes — a dark resinous flagship, a complex green-and-resinous sibling, and a white floral line associated with the elite perfume culture of Cleopatra's Alexandria. Our versions are evidence-based experimental reconstructions, not claims to reproduce one exact ancient bottle. The most important open question is the identity of balanos oil: both desert date (Balanites) and ben-nut (Moringa) remain serious candidates and should be shown as alternatives rather than collapsed into one answer.

    The three perfumes at a glance

    Perfume In simple terms Main ingredients Process in brief
    Dark Mendesian A dark, warm, resinous Egyptian perfume — the flagship of the group. Balanos oil, myrrh, cassia, prepared terebinth product; some recipes add cinnamon. A long cold infusion; the prepared terebinth product is mixed in near the end.
    Metopion A layered green, spicy, and resinous perfume with many more ingredients. Green olive oil, bitter almonds, cardamom, aromatic rush, aromatic reed, myrrh, balsam seed, galbanum, prepared terebinth product, honey, and wine. The dry ingredients steep with gentle heat; the prepared resins are dissolved and the mixture is strained warm.
    Susinum (white Mendesian) A pale lily perfume: floral, labor-intensive, and made by repeatedly loading fresh flowers into oil. Lilies and oil, with aromatic reed, myrrh, cardamom/cinnamon, saffron, honey, gum, water, and salt in the fuller recipe. Successive charges of fresh flowers are pressed and replaced to concentrate the scent.

    Illustrated ingredient catalogue in plain terms

    Balanos oil

    The neutral carrier oil for the dark Mendesian. Its plant identity remains open: desert date (Balanites aegyptiaca) and ben-nut (Moringa) are both serious candidates.

    Used in: Dark Mendesian; a simple Susinum tradition.

    Myrrh

    An aromatic Commiphora gum-resin with a warm, bitter, medicinal smell.

    Used in: All three perfumes.

    Cassia and cinnamon

    Related aromatic barks, treated as distinct ingredients or grades in the ancient recipes.

    Used in: Dark Mendesian; part of the cinnamon/cardamom textual question in Susinum.

    Prepared terebinth product

    A Pistacia resin product cooked down before use. It supplies body and colour rather than a pine-like smell.

    Used in: Dark Mendesian and Metopion.

    Green olive oil and bitter almonds

    The green oil and crushed almond material form Metopion's base. Bitter almonds contain cyanogenic compounds and are not for tasting.

    Used in: Metopion.

    Cardamom

    A pungent, sweet-spicy seed pod; the exact ancient species remains open.

    Used in: Metopion and Susinum.

    Aromatic reed and aromatic rush

    Fragrant reed- or grass-like plants that add a dry, grassy note; the ancient names do not map perfectly onto modern species.

    Used in: Metopion; aromatic reed also in Susinum.

    Galbanum

    A powerful, sharp, green-smelling gum-resin from a Ferula plant.

    Used in: Metopion.

    Balsam seed

    A rare aromatic fruit or seed named in the Metopion recipe. Its exact historical sourcing remains difficult.

    Used in: Metopion.

    Lilies

    The floral heart of Susinum, repeatedly replaced with fresh flowers to build the scent. Madonna lily is the leading historical identification.

    Used in: Susinum.

    Saffron

    Dried Crocus sativus stigmas that contribute aroma and golden colour.

    Used in: Susinum.

    Honey, wine, water, salt, and gum

    Workshop materials that wash, bind, preserve, texture, or otherwise process the aromatics.

    Used in: Metopion and Susinum.

    What this is. You asked for the ingredients of the two fragrances — "Cleopatra's scent" and Susinum. "Cleopatra's perfume" in the press coverage is our reconstruction of the Mendesian, the flagship Egyptian perfume of the Ptolemaic industry; this catalogue gives the actual ancient ingredient lists for it and for Metopion (its Egyptian sibling, transmitted in the same chapter of Dioscorides) and Susinum (the lily perfume the ancient lexica call the white Mendesian), each with precise citations, physical descriptions, and sourcing. One press descriptor needs correcting: "sharp pine" should not be mapped to terebinth. The perfumery ingredient was a cooked, prepared resin product used for body and colour, not a fragrant raw resin. The "desert date oil" is one of the two candidates for balanos oil (the identification is open — see the entry); the myrrh, cinnamon and cardamom are all here, in their places, with their ancient grades.

    The "Cleopatra" question, and the house of Mendes

    No ancient source records Cleopatra's personal perfume. What the sources record is better: a dynasty of Ptolemaic queens as the recognized patrons of the Egyptian perfume industry. Apollonius "Mys," the Herophilean pharmacologist writing in Alexandria within living memory of Cleopatra, says perfume production there "flourished on account of wealth and the devotion (σπουδή) of Arsinoe and Berenice" — the first queens of the dynasty — and that Cyrene's celebrated rose perfume belonged to "the time when Berenice the Great was alive" (Athenaeus, Deipn. 15.38). The same fragment credits Stratonice with raising Adramyttium's vine-flower perfume from "middling" to "first-rate": in the Hellenistic world, royal women made perfume capitals, and the Ptolemaic queens were the most famous practitioners of the art. Cleopatra VII is the last of that line. Apollonius also ranks the products — "Metopion and Mendesion are prepared best in Egypt" — inside a catalogue that is a map of a Mediterranean-wide market of named appellations, and adds the industry's own maxim: it is "the suppliers, the raw material and the craftsmen that make the finest perfume — not the places."

    And one turn more, which organizes everything below: two independent ancient traditions carry the name "Mendesian," and they do not describe the same product. The recipe tradition (Dioscorides, Aëtius, Paul) gives the dark, resinous Mendesian of §1. The lexical tradition knows another: Erotian reports the "Egyptian perfume" of the Hippocratic texts "called by some Malabathrinon, by others the Mendesian," and Galen's Hippocratic glossary gives a matched pair — "White Egyptian oil … also called krininon and susinum oil"; "White Egyptian perfume: which is also called Mendesian, prepared with lilies and aromatics … the same thing is also called lily-perfume and susinum." A white Mendesian, identified with susinum itself; Galen elsewhere even preserves a founder-legend, Megalus of Mendes, for the family. One name, a range of products — a house, not a bottle: a dark resinous flagship and a white floral line. The two fragrances you asked about are, by the ancient lexicographers' own testimony, the dark and the white of one house — and the honest, checkable framing is that the Mendesian is the house scent of Cleopatra's Alexandria, not "Cleopatra's perfume," singular, which no source records and which the story doesn't need.

    Part I — The recipes, with citations and quantities

    1. The dark Mendesian (Μενδήσιον, Mendēsion)

    The name. From Mendes in the Nile Delta. Paul's etymology: "so called because it was discovered in Egypt, where the mendes (ram) is also raised" (7.20.31). Mendes kept its perfume pedigree for centuries — the Suda remembers Manetho, high priest of Mendes, as author of a treatise on preparing kyphi: recipe-writing as a priestly credential.

    Three independent recipe witnesses, in chronological order:

    Witness Date Citation Character
    Dioscorides, MM 1.59.3 1st c. CE Wellmann 55.5–55.10 Qualitative: the classic four ingredients, no weights
    Aëtius, Libr. med. 1.126 6th c. CE Olivieri 63.10–63.13 Qualitative, with the cinnamon variant marked ἔνιοι ("some")
    Paul of Aegina, Epitome 7.20.31 7th c. CE Heiberg 2.388.21–2.389.2 The full workshop recipe, with quantities and timings

    Dioscorides' base list (MM 1.59.3): balanos oil (ἔλαιον βαλάνινον), myrrh (σμύρνα), cassia (κασσία), resin (ῥητίνη). He adds that some makers throw in a little cinnamon after the ingredients are weighed out — "uselessly" (ἀνωφελῶς): "ingredients not worked in together do not give up their power."

    Aëtius (1.126) carries the same four (his base oil is named μυροβαλάνινον, "ben-nut oil" — the same balanos base under its other name) and keeps the cinnamon as a marked variant: "but some, after the astringing-step, also sprinkle in cinnamon."

    Paul's quantified recipe (7.20.31):

    Ingredient Greek Amount Modern equivalent
    Balanos oil ἐλαίου βαλανίνου 10 litrai (another copy: 10 xestai) ~3.3 kg (or ~5.5 L)
    Myrrh σμύρνης 3 ounces ~82 g
    Quill cassia κασσίας σύριγγος 3 ounces ~82 g
    Terebinth resin τερεβινθίνης 1 litra (some: 10) ~327 g
    Cinnamon κιναμώμου 3 ounces ~82 g

    A one-line textual note: the Greek (σμύρνης, κασσίας, σύριγγος ἀνὰ οὐγγίας γ′) can be segmented as either two aromatics ("myrrh; quill cassia — 3 oz each") or three ("myrrh; cassia; syrinx — 3 oz each"). We print the perfumers' reading, quill cassia, since σῦριγξ ("pipe") is the market grade of cassia sold in rolled tubes (see the catalogue entry below); our database records both segmentations.

    Process (Paul 7.20.31): the Mendesian mixture itself is explicitly not boiled (τοῦτο οὐχ ἕψεται). The dry aromatics are put into the oil and stirred for sixty days; then the already cooked and prepared terebinth product, melted for incorporation into a portion of the oil, is added; the whole is stirred seven days more and stored. Paul makes the absence of boiling a class marker elsewhere in the same chapter: some perfumes "do not admit boiling at all, but are only mixed — like the Mendesian" (7.20.2). The distinction matters: the resin product had been prepared by cooking, but the Mendesian maceration was not boiled.

    Color: Pliny's workshop-adulteration catalogue (NH 13) notes that oil cut with Mendesian turns black (with susinum, greasy; with rose, white). This is the dark perfume of the pair.

    The Mendesian in the ledgers. The most celebrated Egyptian perfume of the literary tradition survives in the documentary papyri exactly once — as a stock-book. P.Cair.Zen. 1 59089 (Zenon archive, 258/7 BCE): received at Memphis, "of Mendesian perfume: lead alabastra of one kotylē, 26; of two kotylai, 1; of half a kotylē, 5" — then issued over five months at Alexandria, by name and by the half-kotylē: "to Theudoros the orderly, 1 alabastron of half a kotylē … to Echetodoros the Byzantine, 2 alabastra of one kotylē." Standard vessel sizes, named recipients, a slow issue rate: luxury as controlled inventory.
