Executive summary for Allyson

The story in one paragraph

No ancient source records Cleopatra's personal perfume. The historically secure story is richer: ancient writers identify Mendesian and Metopion as leading Egyptian perfumes, while another tradition calls the lily perfume Susinum the white Mendesian. Together they can be presented as a house of Mendes — a dark resinous flagship, a complex green-and-resinous sibling, and a white floral line associated with the elite perfume culture of Cleopatra's Alexandria. Our versions are evidence-based experimental reconstructions, not claims to reproduce one exact ancient bottle. The most important open question is the identity of balanos oil: both desert date (Balanites) and ben-nut (Moringa) remain serious candidates and should be shown as alternatives rather than collapsed into one answer.

The three perfumes at a glance

Perfume In simple terms Main ingredients Best visual or process story
Dark Mendesian A dark, warm, resinous Egyptian perfume — the flagship of the group. Balanos oil, myrrh, cassia, terebinth resin; some recipes add cinnamon. A long cold infusion, followed by resin; show the two possible balanos oils side by side.
Metopion A layered green, spicy, and resinous perfume with many more ingredients. Green olive oil, bitter almonds, cardamom, aromatic rush, aromatic reed, myrrh, balsam seed, galbanum, terebinth resin, honey, and wine. Soaking and gentle heating, then dissolving the resins and straining the perfume while warm.
Susinum (white Mendesian) A pale lily perfume: floral, labor-intensive, and made by repeatedly loading fresh flowers into oil. Lilies and oil, with aromatic reed, myrrh, cardamom/cinnamon, saffron, honey, gum, water, and salt in the fuller recipe. Repeated flower charges, pressing, and skimming; the sheer quantity of lilies makes the strongest image.

Balanos oil The neutral carrier oil for the dark Mendesian. Its plant identity is still debated. Dark Mendesian; a simple Susinum tradition Photograph both candidates: desert date (Balanites aegyptiaca) and ben-nut (Moringa peregrina or M. oleifera). Desert-date oil is the harder, longer-lead item.
Myrrh An aromatic tree resin with a warm, bitter, medicinal smell. All three perfumes Available from the Prague project stock; visually strong as tears, chunks, and ground resin.
Cassia and cinnamon Related aromatic barks, treated as distinct ingredients or grades in the ancient recipes. Dark Mendesian; cinnamon/cardamom question in Susinum Photograph bark tubes, quills, and powder side by side; keep the labels separate.
Terebinth resin Sticky resin from a Pistacia tree; it gives body and fixes other aromas as well as adding its own scent. Dark Mendesian and Metopion Show the raw resin and the moment it dissolves into warmed oil.
Green olive oil and bitter almonds The green oil and crushed almond material that form Metopion's base. Metopion Bitter almonds are regulated and should be handled as a controlled studio ingredient, not tasted.
Cardamom A pungent, sweet-spicy seed pod. Metopion and Susinum Pods, loose seeds, and crushed material give three useful textures.
Aromatic reed and aromatic rush Fragrant grass- or reed-like plants that add a dry, grassy note. Metopion; aromatic reed in Susinum Ancient plant names do not always map neatly to one modern species, so photograph with precise specimen labels.
Galbanum A sharp, green-smelling gum-resin from a Ferula plant. Metopion A small amount has a powerful smell; the pale-to-amber resin is visually distinctive.
Balsam seed A rare aromatic seed named in the Metopion recipe; exact historical sourcing is difficult. Metopion Contact the project before substituting; provenance matters more than quantity.
Lilies The floral heart of Susinum, repeatedly replaced with fresh flowers to build the scent. Susinum Madonna lily is the leading historical choice. If a commercial lily is used for the shoot, caption it clearly as a visual substitute.
Saffron Dried crocus stigmas that contribute aroma and golden color. Susinum Use whole threads as well as the colored infusion.
Honey, wine, water, salt, and gum Workshop materials: they help wash, bind, preserve, texture, or process the aromatics. Metopion and Susinum Easy to source; best shown in process rather than as hero ingredients.

What this is. You asked for the ingredients of the two fragrances — "Cleopatra's scent" and Susinum. "Cleopatra's perfume" in the press coverage is our reconstruction of the Mendesian, the flagship Egyptian perfume of the Ptolemaic industry; this catalogue gives the actual ancient ingredient lists for it and for Metopion (its Egyptian sibling, transmitted in the same chapter of Dioscorides) and Susinum (the lily perfume the ancient lexica call the white Mendesian), each with precise citations, appearance notes for the camera, and sourcing. The familiar press descriptors map cleanly onto real items below: the "sharp pine" is terebinth resin; the "desert date oil" is one of the two candidates for balanos oil (the identification is open — see the entry); the myrrh, cinnamon and cardamom are all here, in their places, with their ancient grades.

The "Cleopatra" question, and the house of Mendes

No ancient source records Cleopatra's personal perfume. What the sources record is better: a dynasty of Ptolemaic queens as the recognized patrons of the Egyptian perfume industry. Apollonius "Mys," the Herophilean pharmacologist writing in Alexandria within living memory of Cleopatra, says perfume production there "flourished on account of wealth and the devotion (σπουδή) of Arsinoe and Berenice" — the first queens of the dynasty — and that Cyrene's celebrated rose perfume belonged to "the time when Berenice the Great was alive" (Athenaeus, Deipn. 15.38). The same fragment credits Stratonice with raising Adramyttium's vine-flower perfume from "middling" to "first-rate": in the Hellenistic world, royal women made perfume capitals, and the Ptolemaic queens were the most famous practitioners of the art. Cleopatra VII is the last of that line. Apollonius also ranks the products — "Metopion and Mendesion are prepared best in Egypt" — inside a catalogue that is a map of a Mediterranean-wide market of named appellations, and adds the industry's own maxim: it is "the suppliers, the raw material and the craftsmen that make the finest perfume — not the places."

And one turn more, which organizes everything below: two independent ancient traditions carry the name "Mendesian," and they do not describe the same product. The recipe tradition (Dioscorides, Aëtius, Paul) gives the dark, resinous Mendesian of §1. The lexical tradition knows another: Erotian reports the "Egyptian perfume" of the Hippocratic texts "called by some Malabathrinon, by others the Mendesian," and Galen's Hippocratic glossary gives a matched pair — "White Egyptian oil … also called krininon and susinum oil"; "White Egyptian perfume: which is also called Mendesian, prepared with lilies and aromatics … the same thing is also called lily-perfume and susinum." A white Mendesian, identified with susinum itself; Galen elsewhere even preserves a founder-legend, Megalus of Mendes, for the family. One name, a range of products — a house, not a bottle: a dark resinous flagship and a white floral line. The two fragrances you asked about are, by the ancient lexicographers' own testimony, the dark and the white of one house — and the honest, checkable framing is that the Mendesian is the house scent of Cleopatra's Alexandria, not "Cleopatra's perfume," singular, which no source records and which the story doesn't need.
