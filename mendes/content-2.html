The weighed recipe. Dioscorides, Pliny and Aëtius all transmit metopion without weights — the descriptive-perfumery register. But the full workshop formula survives, easy to miss because Paul of Aegina files it under the almond-oil heading: Ἀμυγδάλινον, ὃ καὶ μετώπιον — "Almond oil, which is also metopion" (Epitome 7.20.16, Heiberg 2.385.1–2.385.11), opening with the same galbanum etymology ("the Egyptians, having invented this oil, named it metopion because it takes galbanum"). The only quantified metopion in the record, matching Dioscorides item for item:

Process (Paul 7.20.16): the dry aromatics are soaked in the fragrant wine; the resin and galbanum are ground and dissolved in a portion of the oil, then added to the rest once boiled, and then the honey; everything mixed thoroughly, taken off, and strained while still lukewarm — "for it becomes thick as it cools" (ἕως ἔτι χλιαρόν ἐστι, σειροῦμεν· παχὺ γὰρ γίνεται ψυχόμενον). The mixture therefore had to pass through the sieve before cooling.

Aëtius' list (1.125) matches Dioscorides item for item with one important difference: he omits the myrrh — likely an early copyist's slip, since Dioscorides and Paul both have it. (His spelling σχίνου for the rush is an itacism; Paul's σχοίνου ἄνθους confirms the aromatic rush.)

The name was already disputed in antiquity. Dioscorides: called metopion "because of the admixture of galbanum — the tree from which galbanum comes is called metopon" (MM 1.59.1; Paul repeats the etymology, "the Egyptians, having invented this oil, named it metopion because it takes galbanum," 7.20.16). Pliny attaches the tree-name to a different gum entirely: in his entry on hammoniacum (NH 12.107), the resin that "distills its tear in the sands" of Africa is "produced near the oracle of Hammon by a tree they call metopon" — the namesake tree growing beside the very oracle where Alexander was hailed son of Ammon. And Galen's Hippocratic glossary offers a third derivation: "Egyptian perfume: the perfume from the flowers of the Egyptian thistle, which is also called Metopion." Three authors, three plants — the name was already obscure in antiquity, though galbanum and ammoniacum are at least cousin gum-resins (Dioscorides, for his part, names the ammoniacum shrub agasyllis, keeping metopon for galbanum alone).

Fashion history (Pliny NH 13.6–7). Perfumes rose and fell by city like vintages — iris at Corinth then Cyzicus, rose at Phaselis until Naples and Capua took the crown — and in Egypt "Mendesium and metopium suddenly became more favored; soon Phoenicia took these away," leaving Egypt only the fame of its henna perfume. Even the era's icons were subject to the trend cycle.

The counterfeiter's perfume. Metopion's most vivid appearance in Pliny is not in the perfume book at all but in the balsam chapter (NH 12.123). Balsam of Judaea was the most valuable liquid in the Roman world — sextarii (~0.55 L) bought from the imperial treasury at 300 denarii resold at 1,000: roughly $60,000 wholesale, $200,000 retail at the day's-wage anchor (1st-c. denarius ≈ one day's pay ≈ $200) — and the fraud tests were bench chemistry: genuine balsam dropped in warm water thickens and sinks; adulterated floats like oil; "and if it has been tampered with metopium, it is ringed with a white circle." Dioscorides gives the matching list of balsam adulterants — terebinth-, henna- and mastic-perfume, susinum, balanos oil, metopion: all perfumes themselves. The cheap perfume was the vehicle of the fraud — and metopion was cheap enough, next to balsam, to be worth cutting it with.

And in the ledgers: nothing. Where the Mendesian survives in exactly one papyrus stock-book, metopion survives in none — the sole documentary candidate turns out to be μετωπίας, "high-foreheaded," describing a man in a land register. The great brands lived in the pharmacies and the literature; the warehouses mostly wrote "perfume."

The ancient quality test, verbatim from Dioscorides (MM 1.59.1): "Approved is the kind that is heavy-scented and oily, showing the cardamom and the myrrh more than the galbanum."

3. Susinum (σούσινον, sousinon; also λείρινον / κρίνινον) — the lily perfume

If the dark Mendesian is the house's flagship, this is its white line — the perfume Galen's glossary calls the white Mendesian (see the opening section).

Witness Date Citation Character
Dioscorides, MM 1.52 1st c. CE Wellmann 47.15–49.1 The longest, most detailed perfume recipe he gives; full quantities
Galen, Gloss. Hipp. s.vv. "white Egyptian oil," "white Egyptian perfume" 2nd c. CE Kühn XIX The lexical equation: susinum = the white Mendesian
Paul of Aegina, Epitome 7.20.8 7th c. CE Heiberg 2.383.7–2.383.21 A distinct quantified workshop recipe ("compound susinum")

Dioscorides' manufacture (MM 1.52), stage by stage:

Stage 1 — the charged oil: olive oil, 9 litrai 5 oz (~3.1 kg), boiled with aromatic reed, 5 litrai 10 oz (~1.9 kg), and myrrh, 5 drachms (~17 g), kneaded in fragrant wine; then strained over pounded cardamom soaked in rainwater, 3 litrai 6 oz (~1.1 kg); left to steep, and pressed out.

Stage 2 — the flowers: 3½ litrai (~1.1 kg) of this astringed oil is poured over one thousand stripped lily blossoms laid in a broad, shallow basin (λουτὴρ πλατύς, μὴ βαθύς) — the flowers in a thin bed, the oil sheeting over them — and worked with hands smeared in honey. One day and one night; in the morning the mass is taken up into a wicker basket (κυρτίς) and pressed — the basket is press and strainer in one. Second and third pourings over the same flowers follow, each with fresh pounded cardamom (10 holkai, ~34 g); the first pressing is the best grade, the second next, the third last. Fine salt is sprinkled during clarification and the collected impurity carefully removed; the transfer vessels are honey-coated. Then the whole cycle repeats with a thousand fresh lilies: "however many times you steep fresh lilies, the more potent your perfume."

Stage 3 — the finish, per batch: good myrrh 2 drachms, cinnamon 2 drachms, saffron 10 drachms — but see the crux below. Others (οἱ δέ) finish instead with equal parts saffron and cinnamon, sifted into water in the basin under the first-pressed perfume. Storage jars are pre-coated with gum, myrrh, saffron and honey diluted in water.

The textual crux. In the finishing triad, every Greek manuscript and the early Latin translation read cardamom (καρδαμώμου); the cinnamon (κιναμώμου) that modern editions print is a Renaissance editor's conjecture (Saracenus), adopted by Wellmann (apparatus ad loc.: καρδαμώμου libri et [vet. Lat.]: corr. Sar.). Whether the third finishing aromatic was cardamom or cinnamon is a live textual question in our edition and should remain unresolved in the account.

The simple version, and the best. "Some prepare the simple kind from balanos oil, or another oil, and lilies. That made in Phoenicia and in Egypt is thought to excel; and the best of it is the kind that smells of lilies" (MM 1.52.5). Pliny calls susinum the thinnest and finest of all perfumes.

Paul's compound susinum (7.20.8) — a different, fully quantified workshop recipe, worth its own table because it shows the tradition still alive six centuries after Dioscorides:

Ingredient Greek Amount Modern equivalent
Oil ἐλαίου 3 xestai ~1.7 L
Aromatic reed καλάμου ἀρωματικοῦ 5 litrai ~1.6 kg
Myrrh σμύρνης 5 ounces ~135 g
Cardamom καρδαμώμου 3 litrai ~1 kg
Lily petals, stripped and dried κρίνου τῶν πετάλων ἐξωνυχισμένων καὶ ἐψυγμένων 10 litrai ~3.3 kg
Cinnamon κιναμώμου 3 ounces ~82 g

The lilies go in across three insertions (εἰς τρεῖς ἐμβολάς), each strained off after three days — Dioscorides' repeated-enfleurage principle, systematized. And Paul ends with a substitution clause that is a document of the spice market in one line: the cinnamon may be replaced by arnabo (ἀρναβώ, an aromatic we cannot identify), or by double the amount of cassia, or by karpesion (καρπήσιον). The cinnamon-for-cassia doubling rule is the same one Galen reports for the perfumers.

The name — an Egyptian word in Greek dress. σούσινον is not Greek. The Greeks had a perfectly good word for lily (κρίνον) and did not use it for the perfume: the trade name travelled with the product, most likely from Egyptian zšn ("lotus/lily") through Persian — Athenaeus notes that souson is the Persian word for lily (12.513f) — into Greek. By the 7th century the chain was invisible: Paul guesses the perfume was named after the city of Susa (7.20.7–8), a false etymology. For the story: the name itself is a fossil of the Egyptian perfume industry's dominance — the word travelled with the thing, the way "sake" or "kimchi" do now. For the layout: reliefs in the Louvre show Egyptian women collecting lilies and pressing them into oil — ancient process images of exactly the scenes in Dioscorides' recipe, and a natural pairing for the still lifes.

Who wore it. Theophrastus (De odoribus 42) puts the light florals — rose, kypros, "and, besides these, the lily" — as best suited to men; the heavy, clinging, myrrh-laden Egyptian perfumes were what women wore, "for women seek what lasts" (ζητοῦσι τὰ χρόνια). The gender coding runs opposite to modern expectation.

Part II — Ingredient catalogue

One entry per material, alphabetical by Greek. Each entry gives identity and status, where it appears, a physical description, and sourcing. "Prague stock" means material held by the Alchemies of Scent laboratory, which also guarantees the specimen matches what the chemistry was run on.

ἅλες λεπτοί (hales leptoi) — fine salt

In: susinum (clarification stage, Dsc. MM 1.52.2). Looks like: fine white crystal; ancient salt was sea or lake salt, slightly grey. Sourcing: trivial.

ἀμύγδαλα πικρά (amygdala pikra) — bitter almonds

In: metopion — the pressed oil is the base (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125; Apollonius Mys ap. Athen. 15.38 makes it the perfume's signature). Identification: secure (the bitter variety of the almond). Theophrastus supplies the theory behind the whole oily-base craft: fragrance tracks fat — "all these are fragrant, and roughly all that have some richness and fat; those without fat are scentless" (HP 9.1.3). The fat-rich almond kernel carries scent; its fat-free gum, he notes, is odourless and useless. Looks like: indistinguishable from sweet almonds in shell and kernel — the difference is the taste and chemistry (amygdalin). Sourcing: bitter almonds are food-regulated in the EU (cyanogenic); obtainable from specialist suppliers for non-food use, or available from Prague with the oil pressed there. Safety: not for tasting.

ἀρναβώ (arnabō) — an unidentified aromatic

In: Paul's susinum, as a permitted substitute for cinnamon (7.20.8). Identification: none — the word survives in a handful of late medical texts and no ancient author describes the plant. We do not guess; no reliable visual representation is possible.

βάλανος μυρεψική (balanos myrepsikē) — "the perfumer's acorn/nut"; oil: ἔλαιον βαλάνινον / μυροβαλάνινον

In: the base of the dark Mendesian (all three witnesses) and of simple susinum (Dsc. MM 1.52.5). Identification: genuinely open — the best open question in the piece. Two candidates, Balanites aegyptiaca (desert date) and Moringa (ben-nut), are both attested archaeologically in Egyptian oil production, and no ancient description decides between them. Our laboratory runs both. The ancient descriptions: Dioscorides 4.157 — fruit of a tree "resembling the tamarisk," like a hazelnut; its inside, pressed, gives out a liquid "as bitter almonds do, which they use in costly perfumes in place of oil"; the best is "fresh, full, white, and easily peeled." Pliny NH 12.100–103 grades the market by shell color region by region (best of all the Petraean: black shell, white kernel) and gives the trade secret: "perfumers press only the husks; physicians the kernels too." Price: 2 denarii a pound in Pliny (≈ $400 at the day's-wage anchor) — while myrrh commanded hundreds of denarii: the base was nearly free; the aromatics were the fortune. Looks like: Balanites — a date-like drupe, 2.5–4 cm, yellow-brown, fibrous flesh over a hard ridged stone; Moringa — long ribbed pods to 30+ cm with pale three-winged seeds. The two candidates must remain distinct because the sources do not settle the identification. Sourcing: both candidate materials are in Prague stock. Moringa seed and oil are commercially easy; Balanites fruit is the long-lead item.

βαλσάμου καρπός / καρποβάλσαμον (karpobalsamon) — balsam seed

In: metopion (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125). Identification: the fruit of the balsam of Judaea (conventionally Commiphora gileadensis), the most valuable liquid-producing shrub in the Roman world (Pliny NH 12.111–123). Theophrastus (HP 9.6) knew it three centuries earlier as a plant existing in exactly two cultivated gardens in the Jordan valley — one of about twenty plethra, one much smaller, "nowhere any wild balsam" — a tree the size of a large pomegranate with a whitish rue-like evergreen leaf, incised with iron nails, not knives, in the stifling heat, yielding one shell-measure per man per day; the unmixed resin sold "for twice its weight in silver" and was adulterated before it ever reached Greece. And the detail that matters for this ingredient: the fruit, terebinth-like in size, shape and colour, he calls even more fragrant than the resin (9.6.1) — the perfumers' karpobalsamon was not a substitute for the precious opobalsam but a prized aromatic in its own right. Looks like: small reddish-brown ovoid capsules, a few millimetres, on the dried twig. Sourcing: the hardest item on the list — the plant survives in cultivation (Arabian peninsula; conservation collections in Israel and Jordan). Talk to us before attempting to source; we can advise and may be able to arrange study material.

ἔλαιον (elaion) — olive oil

In: susinum stage 1 (Dsc. MM 1.52); Paul's susinum. Sourcing: trivial; for authenticity, a green early-harvest oil (see omphacine, next).

ἔλαιον ὀμφάκινον (elaion omphakinon) — omphacine oil, "unripe-fruit oil"

In: metopion (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125). Identification: the fine, astringent grade pressed from unripe olives — the prestige perfume base (so also in the amarakinon, Aët. 1.128). Looks like: a distinctly green, slightly cloudy oil. Sourcing: early-harvest ("agourelaio"-style) Greek oil is a fair modern proxy; we press our own — Prague stock.

κάλαμος ἀρωματικός (kalamos arōmatikos) — aromatic reed

In: metopion; susinum stage 1 (charged oil); Paul's susinum at 5 litrai. Identification: conventionally sweet flag (Acorus calamus) rhizome, but "reed" identifications in the aromatics trade are debated; we flag, not settle. Theophrastus (HP 9.7.1) grows it beside the aromatic rush in the Lebanon valley by the lake (see the σχοῖνος entry for his full description): scentless green, fragrant only dried, indistinguishable by eye from common reed — the smell, not the look, is the identity. Looks like: finger-thick dried rhizome sections, tan and wrinkled outside, pale spongy pith inside; warm, sweet-spicy smell. Sourcing: Prague stock (this is one of the four we routinely ship).

καρδάμωμον (kardamōmon) — cardamom

In: metopion (a marker of quality — the approved kind "shows the cardamom"); susinum, in every stage and at the heart of the finishing-triad crux; Paul's susinum at 3 litrai. Identification: an eastern spice of the cardamom/amomum group; whether ancient καρδάμωμον is the modern true cardamom (Elettaria) or a related capsule is not fully settled. Already Theophrastus (HP 9.7.2) could not pin its origin: "As for cardamom and amomum, some say they come from Media, others say from India" — the supply chain was obscure from the start. Ancient handling note: used "cleaned, the pods without the core" — i.e. the husked seed. Looks like: pale green-to-straw three-sided capsules containing dark resinous seeds. Sourcing: any good spice merchant (whole green pods).

καρπήσιον (karpēsion) — karpesion

In: Paul's susinum, third permitted substitute for cinnamon (7.20.8). Identification: a bark/twig aromatic known to Galen (who compares it to valerian-family drugs); no secure modern identity and therefore no reliable visual representation.

κασσία, κασσία σύριγξ (kassia, kassia syrinx) — cassia; "quill cassia"

In: the dark Mendesian in all three witnesses (Paul: 3 oz of the quill grade); cassia at double weight is the perfumers' cinnamon substitute (Paul 7.20.8; Galen). Identification: cinnamon-bark family (cassia group); σῦριγξ, "pipe," is not a plant but a market grade — Galen (De antidotis, Kühn XIV.73) walks the whole hierarchy: gizi (the best, "nearly identical to cinnamon and often sold as it"), moto, arebo, daphnitis, and at the bottom syrinx, "the cheapest of all: its outer bark … is strong in smell and taste, but its inner part is extremely weak." The Mendesian, by Paul's letter, uses the cheap grade — a perfume built on patient time rather than costly bark. Theophrastus (HP 9.5.3) describes the plant and a startling processing step: rods "thicker [than cinnamon], very fibrous," impossible to strip clean — so the cut two-finger lengths are sewn up in fresh-flayed hide, and the worms that breed there eat away the wood but do not touch the bark, "because of the bitterness and pungency of its smell." Quality control by worm. Looks like: thick single-layer rolled bark tubes, coarser and darker than true-cinnamon quills; the rolled form gives the market grade its name. Sourcing: any good spice merchant (sold as cassia or "Chinese cinnamon" quills).

κινάμωμον (kinamōmon) — cinnamon

In: the Mendesian as the disputed fifth ingredient (Dsc.: added "uselessly"; Aët.: sprinkled in by "some" after the astringing; Paul: canonical at 3 oz — watch the recipe harden across six centuries); susinum finishing triad (by editorial conjecture — see the crux) and Paul's susinum at 3 oz. Identification: the ancient cinnamon/cassia distinction does not map neatly onto the modern C. verum / C. cassia split; both ancient terms sit in the cinnamon-bark family. Galen reports the belief that the best cassia turns into true cinnamon at the top of the same bush. Theophrastus (HP 9.5.1–2) already knows the grade logic: a shrub the size of a chaste-tree, cut and divided into five grades by distance from the shoot-tip — best at the tip, worst at the root, "for the bark is what is useful, not the wood." He also transmits, and flatly rejects, the harvest legend: cinnamon growing in ravines full of deadly snakes, the gatherers dividing the crop into three and leaving the Sun's portion, which bursts into flame as they depart — "this one," he says, "is genuinely a myth" (οὗτος μὲν οὖν τῷ ὄντι μῦθος). Looks like: tight multi-layered pale-brown quills (modern "Ceylon" type), visibly finer than cassia tubes. Sourcing: any good spice merchant.
