    • Claims not verified—and therefore not silently plotted as ancient origins

    • En Gedi as the balsam source. Pliny 12.111–123 gives Judaea and two royal gardens but does not name En Gedi; Josephus BJ 4.469 verifies Jericho. En Gedi is omitted.
    • Egyptian origin or pressing of ἔλαιον ὀμφάκινον. The sources place Metopion manufacture/invention in Egypt; they do not say the omphacine oil itself came from Egypt. Its workshop marker is dashed. Pliny 13.8 does separately attest bitter-almond oil expression in Egypt.
    • Local or Aegean origins for ordinary oil, fragrant wine, rainwater, fine salt, gum, and generic Susinum honey. The recipes name or use them but do not state a provenance. Dashed points mark working supply context only.
    • Local lily cultivation. Dioscorides 1.52.5 says the best finished Susinum is made in Phoenicia and Egypt; it does not state where the lilies themselves grew. Product and inferred flower-supply markers are distinguished.
    • ἀρναβώ (arnabō). Paul 7.20.8 transmits it as a Susinum substitute but provides no geographical origin; it remains unlocated in the index.
    • Exact coordinates for Pliny’s interior myrrh grade names (Atramitic, Gebbanitic, Ausaritis, Dianitis, Sambracena, Dusaritis, Mesalum). The names are retained under the Arabian claim, but not assigned invented point coordinates.
    • Which “Bosporus” Dioscorides 1.6 intends. The source’s toponym is plotted approximately at the Cimmerian Bosporus and flagged in its note; the textual referent is not silently disambiguated.
    • A botanical identity for generic ῥητίνη, σύριγξ, καρπήσιον, or Pliny’s metopon. Conventional glosses are supplied for reading; the map does not force modern taxa.

    Part I — The recipes, with citations and quantities

    1. The dark Mendesian (Μενδήσιον, Mendēsion)

    The name. From Mendes in the Nile Delta. Paul's etymology: "so called because it was discovered in Egypt, where the mendes (ram) is also raised" (7.20.31). Mendes kept its perfume pedigree for centuries — the Suda remembers Manetho, high priest of Mendes, as author of a treatise on preparing kyphi: recipe-writing as a priestly credential.

    Three independent recipe witnesses, in chronological order:

    Witness Date Citation Character
    Dioscorides, MM 1.59.3 1st c. CE Wellmann 55.5–55.10 Qualitative: the classic four ingredients, no weights
    Aëtius, Libr. med. 1.126 6th c. CE Olivieri 63.10–63.13 Qualitative, with the cinnamon variant marked ἔνιοι ("some")
    Paul of Aegina, Epitome 7.20.31 7th c. CE Heiberg 2.388.21–2.389.2 The full workshop recipe, with quantities and timings

    Dioscorides' base list (MM 1.59.3): balanos oil (ἔλαιον βαλάνινον), myrrh (σμύρνα), cassia (κασσία), resin (ῥητίνη). He adds that some makers throw in a little cinnamon after the ingredients are weighed out — "uselessly" (ἀνωφελῶς): "ingredients not worked in together do not give up their power."

    Aëtius (1.126) carries the same four (his base oil is named μυροβαλάνινον, "ben-nut oil" — the same balanos base under its other name) and keeps the cinnamon as a marked variant: "but some, after the astringing-step, also sprinkle in cinnamon."

    Paul's quantified recipe (7.20.31):

    Ingredient Greek Amount Modern equivalent
    Balanos oil ἐλαίου βαλανίνου 10 litrai (another copy: 10 xestai) ~3.3 kg (or ~5.5 L)
    Myrrh σμύρνης 3 ounces ~82 g
    Quill cassia κασσίας σύριγγος 3 ounces ~82 g
    Terebinth resin τερεβινθίνης 1 litra (some: 10) ~327 g
    Cinnamon κιναμώμου 3 ounces ~82 g

    A one-line textual note: the Greek (σμύρνης, κασσίας, σύριγγος ἀνὰ οὐγγίας γ′) can be segmented as either two aromatics ("myrrh; quill cassia — 3 oz each") or three ("myrrh; cassia; syrinx — 3 oz each"). We print the perfumers' reading, quill cassia, since σῦριγξ ("pipe") is the market grade of cassia sold in rolled tubes (see the catalogue entry below); our database records both segmentations.

    Process (Paul 7.20.31): explicitly not boiled (τοῦτο οὐχ ἕψεται). The dry aromatics are put into the oil and stirred for sixty days; then the terebinth, melted, is added into a portion of the oil; the whole is stirred seven days more and stored. A cold, slow maceration — two months of patience, no heat. Paul makes this a class marker elsewhere in the same chapter: some perfumes "do not admit boiling at all, but are only mixed — like the Mendesian" (7.20.2). No fire anywhere in the making: the patience was the technique.

    Color: Pliny's workshop-adulteration catalogue (NH 13) notes that oil cut with Mendesian turns black (with susinum, greasy; with rose, white). This is the dark perfume of the pair.

    The Mendesian in the ledgers. The most celebrated Egyptian perfume of the literary tradition survives in the documentary papyri exactly once — as a stock-book. P.Cair.Zen. 1 59089 (Zenon archive, 258/7 BCE): received at Memphis, "of Mendesian perfume: lead alabastra of one kotylē, 26; of two kotylai, 1; of half a kotylē, 5" — then issued over five months at Alexandria, by name and by the half-kotylē: "to Theudoros the orderly, 1 alabastron of half a kotylē … to Echetodoros the Byzantine, 2 alabastra of one kotylē." Standard vessel sizes, named recipients, a slow issue rate: luxury as controlled inventory.

    2. Metopion (μετώπιον, metōpion)

    Witness Date Citation Character
    Dioscorides, MM 1.59.1–2 1st c. CE Wellmann 54.10–55.5 Full ingredient list, quality test, pharmacology; no weights
    Pliny, NH 13.8 1st c. CE — Agreeing list; specifies the resin as terebinth
    Aëtius, Libr. med. 1.125 6th c. CE Olivieri 63.3–63.9 The same recipe still current five centuries on; no weights
    Paul of Aegina, Epitome 7.20.16 7th c. CE Heiberg 2.385.1–2.385.11 The weighed recipe — filed under "Ἀμυγδάλινον, which is also metopion"

    Dioscorides' list (MM 1.59.1) — eleven components, the maximalist against the Mendesian's minimalist five:

    # Ingredient Greek
    1 Bitter almonds (the pressed oil is the base) ἀμύγδαλα πικρά
    2 Omphacine oil (unripe-fruit oil) ἔλαιον ὀμφάκινον
    3 Cardamom καρδάμωμον
    4 Aromatic rush σχοῖνος
    5 Aromatic reed κάλαμος
    6 Honey μέλι
    7 Wine οἶνος
    8 Myrrh σμύρνα
    9 Balsam seed βαλσάμου καρπός
    10 Galbanum χαλβάνη
    11 Resin (terebinth, per Pliny) ῥητίνη

    The weighed recipe. Dioscorides, Pliny and Aëtius all transmit metopion without weights — the descriptive-perfumery register. But the full workshop formula survives, easy to miss because Paul of Aegina files it under the almond-oil heading: Ἀμυγδάλινον, ὃ καὶ μετώπιον — "Almond oil, which is also metopion" (Epitome 7.20.16, Heiberg 2.385.1–2.385.11), opening with the same galbanum etymology ("the Egyptians, having invented this oil, named it metopion because it takes galbanum"). The only quantified metopion in the record, matching Dioscorides item for item:

    Ingredient Greek Amount Modern equivalent
    Omphacine oil (the base) ἐλαίου ὀμφακίνου 20 xestai ~11 L
    Bitter almonds ἀμυγδάλων πικρῶν 2 litrai ~650 g
    Cardamom καρδαμώμου 1 litra ~327 g
    Rush-flower σχοίνου ἄνθους 1 litra (ἀνά, "each") ~327 g
    Aromatic reed καλάμου ἀρωματικοῦ 1 litra (each) ~327 g
    Balsam seed καρποβαλσάμου 1 litra (each) ~327 g
    Myrrh σμύρνης 6 ounces (ἀνά, "each") ~164 g
    Galbanum χαλβάνης 6 ounces (each) ~164 g
    Terebinth resin τερεβινθίνης 2 litrai ~650 g
    Fragrant wine, "for soaking the dry ingredients" οἴνου εὐώδους εἰς τὸ ἐμβρέξαι τὰ ξηρά 4 xestai ~2.2 L
    Attic honey μέλιτος Ἀττικοῦ 3 litrai ~1 kg

    Process (Paul 7.20.16): the dry aromatics are soaked in the fragrant wine; the resin and galbanum are ground and dissolved in a portion of the oil, then added to the rest once boiled, and then the honey; everything mixed thoroughly, taken off, and strained while still lukewarm — "for it becomes thick as it cools" (ἕως ἔτι χλιαρόν ἐστι, σειροῦμεν· παχὺ γὰρ γίνεται ψυχόμενον). A caption-ready workshop detail: this was a perfume you had to race through a sieve.

    Aëtius' list (1.125) matches Dioscorides item for item with one difference worth a caption: he omits the myrrh — likely an early copyist's slip, since Dioscorides and Paul both have it. (His spelling σχίνου for the rush is an itacism; Paul's σχοίνου ἄνθους confirms the aromatic rush.)

    The name was already disputed in antiquity. Dioscorides: called metopion "because of the admixture of galbanum — the tree from which galbanum comes is called metopon" (MM 1.59.1; Paul repeats the etymology, "the Egyptians, having invented this oil, named it metopion because it takes galbanum," 7.20.16). Pliny attaches the tree-name to a different gum entirely: in his entry on hammoniacum (NH 12.107), the resin that "distills its tear in the sands" of Africa is "produced near the oracle of Hammon by a tree they call metopon" — the namesake tree growing beside the very oracle where Alexander was hailed son of Ammon. And Galen's Hippocratic glossary offers a third derivation: "Egyptian perfume: the perfume from the flowers of the Egyptian thistle, which is also called Metopion." Three authors, three plants — the name was already obscure in antiquity, though galbanum and ammoniacum are at least cousin gum-resins (Dioscorides, for his part, names the ammoniacum shrub agasyllis, keeping metopon for galbanum alone).

    Fashion history (Pliny NH 13.6–7). Perfumes rose and fell by city like vintages — iris at Corinth then Cyzicus, rose at Phaselis until Naples and Capua took the crown — and in Egypt "Mendesium and metopium suddenly became more favored; soon Phoenicia took these away," leaving Egypt only the fame of its henna perfume. Even the era's icons were subject to the trend cycle.

    The counterfeiter's perfume. Metopion's most vivid appearance in Pliny is not in the perfume book at all but in the balsam chapter (NH 12.123). Balsam of Judaea was the most valuable liquid in the Roman world — sextarii (~0.55 L) bought from the imperial treasury at 300 denarii resold at 1,000: roughly $60,000 wholesale, $200,000 retail at the day's-wage anchor (1st-c. denarius ≈ one day's pay ≈ $200) — and the fraud tests were bench chemistry: genuine balsam dropped in warm water thickens and sinks; adulterated floats like oil; "and if it has been tampered with metopium, it is ringed with a white circle." Dioscorides gives the matching list of balsam adulterants — terebinth-, henna- and mastic-perfume, susinum, balanos oil, metopion: all perfumes themselves. The cheap perfume was the vehicle of the fraud — and metopion was cheap enough, next to balsam, to be worth cutting it with.

    And in the ledgers: nothing. Where the Mendesian survives in exactly one papyrus stock-book, metopion survives in none — the sole documentary candidate turns out to be μετωπίας, "high-foreheaded," describing a man in a land register. The great brands lived in the pharmacies and the literature; the warehouses mostly wrote "perfume."

    The ancient quality test, verbatim from Dioscorides (MM 1.59.1): "Approved is the kind that is heavy-scented and oily, showing the cardamom and the myrrh more than the galbanum." Usable as a caption without alteration.

    3. Susinum (σούσινον, sousinon; also λείρινον / κρίνινον) — the lily perfume

    If the dark Mendesian is the house's flagship, this is its white line — the perfume Galen's glossary calls the white Mendesian (see the opening section).

    Witness Date Citation Character
    Dioscorides, MM 1.52 1st c. CE Wellmann 47.15–49.1 The longest, most detailed perfume recipe he gives; full quantities
    Galen, Gloss. Hipp. s.vv. "white Egyptian oil," "white Egyptian perfume" 2nd c. CE Kühn XIX The lexical equation: susinum = the white Mendesian
    Paul of Aegina, Epitome 7.20.8 7th c. CE Heiberg 2.383.7–2.383.21 A distinct quantified workshop recipe ("compound susinum")

    Dioscorides' manufacture (MM 1.52), stage by stage:

    Stage 1 — the charged oil: olive oil, 9 litrai 5 oz (~3.1 kg), boiled with aromatic reed, 5 litrai 10 oz (~1.9 kg), and myrrh, 5 drachms (~17 g), kneaded in fragrant wine; then strained over pounded cardamom soaked in rainwater, 3 litrai 6 oz (~1.1 kg); left to steep, and pressed out.

    Stage 2 — the flowers: 3½ litrai (~1.1 kg) of this astringed oil is poured over one thousand stripped lily blossoms laid in a broad, shallow basin (λουτὴρ πλατύς, μὴ βαθύς) — the flowers in a thin bed, the oil sheeting over them — and worked with hands smeared in honey. One day and one night; in the morning the mass is taken up into a wicker basket (κυρτίς) and pressed — the basket is press and strainer in one. Second and third pourings over the same flowers follow, each with fresh pounded cardamom (10 holkai, ~34 g); the first pressing is the best grade, the second next, the third last. Fine salt is sprinkled during clarification and the collected impurity carefully removed; the transfer vessels are honey-coated. Then the whole cycle repeats with a thousand fresh lilies: "however many times you steep fresh lilies, the more potent your perfume."

    Stage 3 — the finish, per batch: good myrrh 2 drachms, cinnamon 2 drachms, saffron 10 drachms — but see the crux below. Others (οἱ δέ) finish instead with equal parts saffron and cinnamon, sifted into water in the basin under the first-pressed perfume. Storage jars are pre-coated with gum, myrrh, saffron and honey diluted in water.

    The crux, for the shoot. In the finishing triad, every Greek manuscript and the early Latin translation read cardamom (καρδαμώμου); the cinnamon (κιναμώμου) that modern editions print is a Renaissance editor's conjecture (Saracenus), adopted by Wellmann (apparatus ad loc.: καρδαμώμου libri et [vet. Lat.]: corr. Sar.). Whether the third finishing aromatic was cardamom or cinnamon is a live textual question in our edition. Photograph both, side by side — the disagreement is six centuries old, and it is the story.

    The simple version, and the best. "Some prepare the simple kind from balanos oil, or another oil, and lilies. That made in Phoenicia and in Egypt is thought to excel; and the best of it is the kind that smells of lilies" (MM 1.52.5). Pliny calls susinum the thinnest and finest of all perfumes.

    Paul's compound susinum (7.20.8) — a different, fully quantified workshop recipe, worth its own table because it shows the tradition still alive six centuries after Dioscorides:

    Ingredient Greek Amount Modern equivalent
    Oil ἐλαίου 3 xestai ~1.7 L
    Aromatic reed καλάμου ἀρωματικοῦ 5 litrai ~1.6 kg
    Myrrh σμύρνης 5 ounces ~135 g
    Cardamom καρδαμώμου 3 litrai ~1 kg
    Lily petals, stripped and dried κρίνου τῶν πετάλων ἐξωνυχισμένων καὶ ἐψυγμένων 10 litrai ~3.3 kg
    Cinnamon κιναμώμου 3 ounces ~82 g
    Cilician saffron κρόκου Κιλικίου 5 ounces ~135 g

    The lilies go in across three insertions (εἰς τρεῖς ἐμβολάς), each strained off after three days — Dioscorides' repeated-enfleurage principle, systematized. And Paul ends with a substitution clause that is a document of the spice market in one line: the cinnamon may be replaced by arnabo (ἀρναβώ, an aromatic we cannot identify), or by double the amount of cassia, or by karpesion (καρπήσιον). The cinnamon-for-cassia doubling rule is the same one Galen reports for the perfumers.

    The name — an Egyptian word in Greek dress. σούσινον is not Greek. The Greeks had a perfectly good word for lily (κρίνον) and did not use it for the perfume: the trade name travelled with the product, most likely from Egyptian zšn ("lotus/lily") through Persian — Athenaeus notes that souson is the Persian word for lily (12.513f) — into Greek. By the 7th century the chain was invisible: Paul guesses the perfume was named after the city of Susa (7.20.7–8), a false etymology. For the story: the name itself is a fossil of the Egyptian perfume industry's dominance — the word travelled with the thing, the way "sake" or "kimchi" do now. For the layout: reliefs in the Louvre show Egyptian women collecting lilies and pressing them into oil — ancient process images of exactly the scenes in Dioscorides' recipe, and a natural pairing for the still lifes.

    Who wore it. Theophrastus (De odoribus 42) puts the light florals — rose, kypros, "and, besides these, the lily" — as best suited to men; the heavy, clinging, myrrh-laden Egyptian perfumes were what women wore, "for women seek what lasts" (ζητοῦσι τὰ χρόνια). The gender coding runs opposite to modern expectation.

    Part II — Ingredient catalogue

    One entry per material, alphabetical by Greek. Each entry: identity and status, where it appears, what it looks like (for the camera), and sourcing. "Prague stock" = we can ship from the Alchemies of Scent laboratory stock, which also guarantees the specimen matches what the chemistry was run on.

    ἅλες λεπτοί (hales leptoi) — fine salt

    In: susinum (clarification stage, Dsc. MM 1.52.2). Looks like: fine white crystal; ancient salt was sea or lake salt, slightly grey. Sourcing: trivial; unrefined sea salt photographs better than table salt.

    ἀμύγδαλα πικρά (amygdala pikra) — bitter almonds

    In: metopion — the pressed oil is the base (Dsc. MM 1.59.1; Aët. 1.125; Apollonius Mys ap. Athen. 15.38 makes it the perfume's signature). Identification: secure (the bitter variety of the almond). Theophrastus supplies the theory behind the whole oily-base craft: fragrance tracks fat — "all these are fragrant, and roughly all that have some richness and fat; those without fat are scentless" (HP 9.1.3). The fat-rich almond kernel carries scent; its fat-free gum, he notes, is odourless and useless. Looks like: indistinguishable from sweet almonds in shell and kernel — the difference is the taste and chemistry (amygdalin). For the shoot: whole drupes, shelled kernels, and a small dish of the expressed oil. Sourcing: bitter almonds are food-regulated in the EU (cyanogenic); obtainable from specialist suppliers for non-food use, or we supply from Prague with the oil we pressed. Safety note for the set: display only, not for tasting.

    ἀρναβώ (arnabō) — an unidentified aromatic

    In: Paul's susinum, as a permitted substitute for cinnamon (7.20.8). Identification: none — the word survives in a handful of late medical texts and no ancient author describes the plant. We do not guess. For the shoot: honestly unphotographable; a nice caption fact ("one ingredient in the substitution clause cannot be identified at all").

    βάλανος μυρεψική (balanos myrepsikē) — "the perfumer's acorn/nut"; oil: ἔλαιον βαλάνινον / μυροβαλάνινον
